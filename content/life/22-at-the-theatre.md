# At the theatre

Going to the theatre in Athens meant a whole day out, from dawn, on a stone or wooden seat in the open air with thousands of others, at a festival for the god Dionysus. The audience ate, shouted, applauded and booed, and the state gave poorer citizens the price of a ticket. Comedy mocked real people by name, some of them sitting in the audience.

---

## A festival, not a night out

Plays were not performed all year round. At Athens they were part of festivals of Dionysus, above all the City Dionysia in spring, when three tragic poets each staged three tragedies and a satyr play, one a day, and five comic poets (fewer in wartime) a comedy each. It was a competition, judged by citizens chosen by lot, and the prize was glory: a rich citizen, the χορηγός, paid for the chorus's costumes and training as a public duty, and a winner might put up a monument. One still stands in Athens, the choregic monument of Lysicrates.

The Theatre of Dionysus on the slope of the Acropolis held many thousands: estimates run from about six thousand to seventeen thousand, depending on the date and on who is counting. When the young poet Agathon won in 416 BCE, Socrates teased him that his wisdom had shone out

> ἐν μάρτυσι τῶν Ἑλλήνων πλέον ἢ τρισμυρίοις
> "before more than thirty thousand Greeks as witnesses."
> ([Plato, *Symposium* 175e](read:tlg0059.tlg011:175e))

That is Socrates' exaggeration, or Plato's; but it gives the scale.

## Tickets and snacks

A seat is said to have cost two obols. From some point in the fifth or fourth century the city handed out a "viewing fund", the θεωρικόν, to pay poorer citizens' way in; [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) repeats the charge that Pericles began it to win the people over, one of many "payments" his critics blamed him for ([*Pericles* 9.1](read:tlg0007.tlg012:9.1)). The fund became so popular that later politicians feared to touch it even in wartime.

People brought food. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086), explaining why one pleasure drives out another, reaches for a theatre example:

> οἷον καὶ ἐν τοῖς θεάτροις οἱ τραγηματίζοντες, ὅταν φαῦλοι οἱ ἀγωνιζόμενοι ὦσι, τότε μάλιστʼ αὐτὸ δρῶσιν.
> "Like the people in the theatres who eat sweets: they do it most when the actors are bad."
> ([Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 10.5](read:tlg0086.tlg010:10.5))

Comic poets bribed the audience with nuts and figs thrown from the stage. [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019) mocks the habit, and does it anyway:

> οὐ γὰρ πρεπῶδές ἐστι τῷ διδασκάλῳ ἰσχάδια καὶ τρωγάλια τοῖς θεωμένοις προβαλόντʼ ἐπὶ τούτοις εἶτʼ ἀναγκάζειν γελᾶν.
> "It's not proper for the author to throw dried figs and nibbles to the audience and then use them to force a laugh."
> ([Aristophanes, *Wealth* 797–799](read:tlg0019.tlg011:797))

Were women in the audience? Nobody is sure, and scholars have argued it both ways for a century.

## Masks and three actors

All the performers were men, citizens, and all wore masks, which let one actor play several parts, women included. The chorus sang and danced in the circular dancing floor, the ὀρχήστρα; the actors spoke in front of a wooden stage building, the σκηνή, which gives us *scene*. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086) gives the history of the cast in one sentence:

> Καὶ τό τε τῶν ὑποκριτῶν πλῆθος ἐξ ἑνὸς εἰς δύο πρῶτος Αἰσχύλος ἤγαγε, καὶ τὰ τοῦ χοροῦ ἠλάττωσε, καὶ τὸν λόγον πρωταγωνιστὴν παρεσκεύασεν· τρεῖς δὲ καὶ σκηνογραφίαν Σοφοκλῆς.
> "Aeschylus first raised the number of actors from one to two, reduced the chorus's part, and made the spoken dialogue the leading element; Sophocles brought in the third actor and scene-painting."
> ([Aristotle, *Poetics* 4](read:tlg0086.tlg034:4.16))

Three actors, however many characters: that rule shapes every surviving Greek tragedy. Stage machinery let gods appear on a crane, the μηχανή, hence *deus ex machina*, "the god from the machine".

## Too close to home

Tragedy usually told old myths, and there was a reason. Early in the century, when the tragedian Phrynichus staged the recent capture of Miletus by the Persians, a city Athens had failed to help, [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) says

> ἐς δάκρυά τε ἔπεσε τὸ θέητρον, καὶ ἐζημίωσάν μιν ὡς ἀναμνήσαντα οἰκήια κακὰ χιλίῃσι δραχμῇσι, καὶ ἐπέταξαν μηδένα χρᾶσθαι τούτῳ τῷ δράματι.
> "the whole theatre burst into tears, and they fined him a thousand drachmas for reminding them of their own misfortunes, and ordered that no one should ever use that play again."
> ([Herodotus 6.21.2](read:tlg0016.tlg001:6.21.2))

## Socrates stands up

Comedy did the opposite: it put real, living people on stage and mocked them by name. Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BCE) turned Socrates into a crank who runs a school of nonsense and floats in a basket to study the sky. Twenty-four years later, at his trial, Socrates told the jury the play was part of the prejudice against him:

> ταῦτα γὰρ ἑωρᾶτε καὶ αὐτοὶ ἐν τῇ Ἀριστοφάνους κωμῳδίᾳ, Σωκράτη τινὰ ἐκεῖ περιφερόμενον, φάσκοντά τε ἀεροβατεῖν καὶ ἄλλην πολλὴν φλυαρίαν φλυαροῦντα
> "You saw it yourselves in Aristophanes' comedy: a Socrates swinging around up there, claiming he was walking on air and talking a great deal of other nonsense."
> ([Plato, *Apology* 19c](read:tlg0059.tlg002:19c))

A later story, told by [Aelian](author:tlg0545), says Socrates was in the audience at the first performance. When foreigners in the crowd started asking which one was the real Socrates, he stood up and remained standing for the whole play, so that everyone could compare him with the mask ([Aelian, *Historical Miscellany* 2.13](read:tlg0545.tlg002:2.13)). It is told six centuries later and may be invented, but the mask makers, Aelian says, had made the likeness as close as they could.

Other stories were told about the audience. The Furies in Aeschylus' *Eumenides* were said to have been so frightening that boys fainted and women miscarried; that comes from a late biography and is almost certainly legend.
