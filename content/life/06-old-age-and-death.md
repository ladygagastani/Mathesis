# Old age and death

The Greeks dreaded old age, honoured the old, and went to great lengths for their dead: washing and laying out the body, lamenting, carrying it out before dawn, and bringing food and oil to the grave for years after. The afterlife they imagined was mostly grey.

---

## Getting old

Lyric poets complained bitterly about old age, but not everyone did. At the start of [Plato](author:tlg0059)'s *Republic* the old businessman Cephalus remembers someone asking the poet Sophocles, by then an old man, about his love life:

> εὐφήμει, ἔφη, ὦ ἄνθρωπε· ἁσμενέστατα μέντοι αὐτὸ ἀπέφυγον, ὥσπερ λυττῶντά τινα καὶ ἄγριον δεσπότην ἀποδράς.
> "'Hush, man,' he said. 'I am delighted to have escaped it, like a slave who has got away from a mad and savage master.'"
> ([Plato, *Republic* 1.329c](read:tlg0059.tlg030:1.329c))

A later story says Sophocles' sons took him to court to have him declared senile and unfit to manage his property, and that he answered by reading the jury a chorus from the play he was writing, *Oedipus at Colonus*, and was acquitted. It is almost certainly a legend, but a telling one.

Caring for aged parents was a legal duty. At Athens a son who failed to feed and house his parents could be prosecuted, and a man chosen for high office was asked in public whether he "treats his parents well" (see [The household](01-the-household.md)).

## The funeral

When someone died, the women of the family closed the eyes and mouth, washed the body, anointed it and dressed it, and laid it out on a bier in the house, feet towards the door. This was the πρόθεσις, the laying out, and relatives came to mourn. A jar of water stood outside the door so that visitors could purify themselves on leaving. A law quoted by [Demosthenes](author:tlg0014) sets out what came next:

> τὸν ἀποθανόντα προτίθεσθαι ἔνδον, ὅπως ἂν βούληται. ἐκφέρειν δὲ τὸν ἀποθανόντα τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ ᾗ ἂν προθῶνται, πρὶν ἥλιον ἐξέχειν. βαδίζειν δὲ τοὺς ἄνδρας πρόσθεν, ὅταν ἐκφέρωνται, τὰς δὲ γυναῖκας ὄπισθεν.
> "The dead person is to be laid out indoors, in whatever way the family wishes, and carried out on the day after the laying out, before the sun is up. The men are to walk in front when the body is carried out, the women behind."
> ([Demosthenes 43.62](read:tlg0014.tlg043:62))

The same law bars women under sixty from the procession unless they were close relatives. Such rules were old. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) says Solon had already tried to rein in displays of grief:

> ἀμυχὰς δὲ κοπτομένων καὶ τὸ θρηνεῖν πεποιημένα καὶ τὸ κωκύειν ἄλλον ἐν ταφαῖς ἑτέρων ἀφεῖλεν. ἐναγίζειν δὲ βοῦν οὐκ εἴασεν, οὐδὲ συντιθέναι πλέον ἱματίων τριῶν
> "He abolished tearing the cheeks in mourning, set dirges, and wailing for anyone at another person's funeral. He did not allow an ox to be sacrificed at the grave, nor more than three garments to be buried with the dead."
> ([Plutarch, *Solon* 21.4](read:tlg0007.tlg007:21.4))

Laws like this were aimed less at grief than at rich families turning funerals into political shows.

## A coin for the ferryman

The dead crossed the river of the underworld in Charon's boat, and the fare, by a custom that shows up in some graves but not most, was a coin placed in the mouth. [Lucian](author:tlg0062) mocks it:

> πρῶτα μὲν φέροντες ὀβολὸν εἰς τὸ στόμα κατέθηκαν αὐτῷ, μισθὸν τῷ πορθμεῖ τῆς ναυτιλίας γενησόμενον, οὐ πρότερον ἐξετάσαντες ὁποῖον τὸ νόμισμα νομίζεται καὶ διαχωρεῖ παρὰ τοῖς κάτω
> "First they put an obol in the dead man's mouth, to pay the ferryman for the crossing, without first finding out what money is current down there and whether it will pass."
> ([Lucian, *On Funerals* 10](read:tlg0062.tlg036:10))

In [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019)' *Frogs* the fare is two obols, a joke about the two-obol payments that Athens handed out to its citizens:

> ἀνὴρ γέρων ναύτης διάξει δύʼ ὀβολὼ μισθὸν λαβών. — φεῦ, ὡς μέγα δύνασθον πανταχοῦ τὼ δύʼ ὀβολώ.
> "An old sailor will ferry you across, for a fare of two obols." "Wow, what power two obols have everywhere!"
> ([Aristophanes, *Frogs* 139–141](read:tlg0019.tlg009:139))

## The grey place

Homer's underworld is no reward. When Odysseus visits it and praises Achilles, the greatest of the heroes, the dead man answers:

> βουλοίμην κʼ ἐπάρουρος ἐὼν θητευέμεν ἄλλῳ, ἀνδρὶ παρʼ ἀκλήρῳ, ᾧ μὴ βίοτος πολὺς εἴη, ἢ πᾶσιν νεκύεσσι καταφθιμένοισιν ἀνάσσειν.
> "I would rather be a hired hand on the land of another man, some poor man without land of his own, than be king over all the dead who have perished."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 11.489–491](read:tlg0012.tlg002:11.489))

The mysteries promised initiates something better (see [Mysteries and oracles](13-mysteries-and-oracles.md)), and philosophers argued for an immortal soul. But the ordinary dead needed the living. Families brought honey, milk, wine and oil to the grave on set days; a tomb neglected was a disgrace.

## Two deaths, two letters

Socrates, condemned to drink hemlock in 399 BCE, spent his last day talking about the soul. His last words, in [Plato](author:tlg0059)'s account, were a small practical request:

> ὦ Κρίτων, ἔφη, τῷ Ἀσκληπιῷ ὀφείλομεν ἀλεκτρυόνα· ἀλλὰ ἀπόδοτε καὶ μὴ ἀμελήσητε.
> "Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it, and don't forget."
> ([Plato, *Phaedo* 118a](read:tlg0059.tlg004:118))

Asclepius was the god of healing, and people have argued ever since about what Socrates meant: that death was a cure for life, or simply that he had a debt to pay.

Five centuries later, Plutarch wrote to his wife after their two-year-old daughter Timoxena died while he was away. He remembers how the little girl would tell her nurse to offer the breast not only to other babies but also to her toys, as if inviting them to her own table, and asks his wife not to let the memory of such things turn to pain. The letter, [*Consolation to his Wife*](read:tlg0007.tlg111), is one of the most tender things to survive from antiquity.
