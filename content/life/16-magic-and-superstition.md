# Magic and superstition

Alongside the gods of the city there was a whole world of spells, curses, omens and charms. Greeks wrote curses on lead and dropped them into graves and wells, stuck pins in wax dolls, bowed when someone sneezed, and turned back if a weasel crossed the road. The same Greeks also wrote the first satires on all of it.

---

## Curse tablets

More than sixteen hundred Greek and Latin curse tablets have been found, most of them thin sheets of lead, inscribed, rolled or folded, often pierced with a nail, and put where they would reach the powers below: in graves, wells, springs and sanctuaries of the underworld gods. The Greeks called them κατάδεσμοι, "binding spells". They "bind" a rival's tongue before a lawsuit, a competitor's hands in business, a charioteer's horses before a race, a lover's desire for anyone else. A typical formula lists body parts one by one: "I bind her tongue, her hands, her feet…" Some come with a small lead or clay doll, its arms tied behind its back.

You could hire a professional. [Plato](author:tlg0059) is scornful of the begging priests and seers who

> ἐπὶ πλουσίων θύρας ἰόντες πείθουσιν ὡς ἔστι παρὰ σφίσι δύναμις ἐκ θεῶν ποριζομένη θυσίαις τε καὶ ἐπῳδαῖς … ἐάν τέ τινα ἐχθρὸν πημῆναι ἐθέλῃ, μετὰ σμικρῶν δαπανῶν ὁμοίως δίκαιον ἀδίκῳ βλάψει ἐπαγωγαῖς τισιν καὶ καταδέσμοις
> "go to rich men's doors and persuade them that they have a power from the gods, got by sacrifices and spells … and that if a man wants to harm an enemy, for a small fee he can hurt a just man as easily as an unjust one, with certain charms and binding spells."
> ([Plato, *Republic* 2.364b–c](read:tlg0059.tlg030:2.364b))

In the *Laws* he admits that such things frighten people whether or not they work, and mentions the wax figures that people find

> εἴτʼ ἐπὶ θύραις εἴτʼ ἐπὶ τριόδοις εἴτʼ ἐπὶ μνήμασι γονέων αὐτῶν
> "on their doors, or at crossroads, or on their parents' tombs."
> ([Plato, *Laws* 11.933b](read:tlg0059.tlg034:11.933b))

His answer is a law: anyone who harms people by binding spells, charms or incantations is to be tried, and if he is a professional prophet or diviner, put to death.

## The superstitious man

[Theophrastus](author:tlg0093), Aristotle's successor, wrote thirty sketches of irritating types, the *Characters*. His superstitious man, the δεισιδαίμων, "the one who fears the spirits", is a small catalogue of Greek folk belief:

> καὶ τὴν ὁδὸν ἐὰν ὑπερδράμῃ γαλῆ, μὴ πρότερον πορευθῆναι, ἕως διεξέλθῃ τις ἢ λίθους τρεῖς ὑπὲρ τῆς ὁδοῦ διαβάλῃ.
> "If a weasel runs across his path, he will not go on until someone else has passed, or he has thrown three stones across the road."
> ([Theophrastus, *Characters* 16](read:tlg0093.tlg009:16.3))

He also pours oil on the smooth stones at crossroads and kneels to them; if a mouse gnaws a hole in his flour sack, he goes to an expert to ask what to do, and when told to have it patched, performs a purification instead; he avoids stepping on a tomb or visiting a woman in childbirth, for fear of pollution; and after a bad dream he consults dream interpreters, seers and bird-watchers to find out which god to pray to.

[Plutarch](author:tlg0007) went further in an essay [*On Superstition*](read:tlg0007.tlg080), arguing that fear of the gods is worse than not believing in them at all: the atheist merely thinks there are no gods, the superstitious man thinks they are cruel.

## Omens

Signs were everywhere: birds, thunder, dreams, the organs of sacrificed animals, chance words overheard. A sneeze was an omen from Zeus. When [Xenophon](author:tlg0032) was urging the Greek mercenaries, stranded deep in Persia, not to give up hope,

> τοῦτο δὲ λέγοντος αὐτοῦ πτάρνυταί τις· ἀκούσαντες δʼ οἱ στρατιῶται πάντες μιᾷ ὁρμῇ προσεκύνησαν τὸν θεόν
> "as he said this, someone sneezed; and when the soldiers heard it, all of them with one impulse bowed down to the god."
> ([Xenophon, *Anabasis* 3.2.9](read:tlg0032.tlg006:3.2.9))

Xenophon, a practical man, used the moment to propose a vow and a vote. Dreams had their own experts: the *Interpretation of Dreams* by [Artemidorus](author:tlg0553), from the second century CE, classifies a great many of them, with case histories.

## The sorcerer's apprentice

The story Goethe turned into a poem and Disney into a cartoon with Mickey Mouse is Greek. It comes from [Lucian](author:tlg0062)'s *Lover of Lies*, a satire in which a group of learned men try to outdo each other with ghost stories. One tells how he travelled in Egypt with a sorcerer, Pancrates, who at every inn would dress up a broom or a pestle, say a spell, and have it fetch water, shop and cook. One day the narrator overheard the spell and tried it himself:

> ἐγὼ δὲ ἀμηχανῶν τῷ πράγματι … ἀξίνην λαβὼν διακόπτω τὸ ὕπερον εἰς δύο μέρη· τὰ δέ, ἑκάτερον τὸ μέρος, ἀμφορέας λαβόντα ὑδροφόρει καὶ ἀνθʼ ἑνὸς δύο μοι ἐγεγένηντο οἱ διάκονοι.
> "At my wits' end … I took an axe and chopped the pestle in two. But each half picked up a jar and carried water, and instead of one servant I now had two."
> ([Lucian, *Lover of Lies* 36](read:tlg0062.tlg031:36))

The pestle had already flooded the house, because he did not know the spell to stop it. Pancrates came back, turned the pieces back into wood, and vanished. Lucian's point is that clever people will believe anything if it is told well.
