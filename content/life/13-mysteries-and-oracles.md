# Mysteries and oracles

Beyond the public sacrifices, Greeks could be initiated into secret rites that promised a better lot after death, and could ask the gods about the future at an oracle. The secret of Eleusis was kept for about a thousand years; the oracle at Delphi was consulted by farmers and kings, and was famous for answers that could be read two ways.

---

## Eleusis: a secret kept

Every autumn thousands walked the twenty kilometres from Athens to Eleusis along the Sacred Way to be initiated into the Mysteries of Demeter and her daughter Persephone. Anyone who spoke Greek and had not committed murder could take part: men and women, slaves and foreigners. What happened inside the great hall, the Telesterion, was never to be told, and it never was. We know about the fasting, the bathing in the sea with a piglet to be sacrificed, the barley drink (κυκεών), the torches, and that something was shown. We do not know what.

The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*, which tells how the goddess lost her daughter to the god of the dead and founded the rites at Eleusis, states the promise:

> ὄλβιος, ὃς τάδʼ ὄπωπεν ἐπιχθονίων ἀνθρώπων· ὃς δʼ ἀτελὴς ἱερῶν ὅς τʼ ἄμμορος, οὔποθʼ ὁμοίων αἶσαν ἔχει φθίμενός περ ὑπὸ ζόφῳ εὐρώεντι.
> "Blessed among men on earth is the one who has seen these things. But the uninitiated, who has no share in the rites, never has the same lot once dead, down in the mouldy dark."
> ([*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 480–482](read:tlg0013.tlg002:480))

Revealing the Mysteries was a capital crime. The playwright Aeschylus was said to have been put on trial for giving away something of them in a play, and to have got off by saying he did not know it was secret. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086) mentions it as an example of an act done in ignorance:

> ἢ οὐκ εἰδέναι ὅτι ἀπόρρητα ἦν, ὥσπερ Αἰσχύλος τὰ μυστικά
> "or not knowing that it was forbidden to tell, as Aeschylus said about the mysteries."
> ([Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 3.1](read:tlg0086.tlg010:3.1))

In 415 BCE Alcibiades and his friends were accused of performing a mock version of the Mysteries at private parties, which is one reason he fled Athens and changed sides.

Modern theories about what was shown include an ear of grain, a sacred drama, or a drug in the kykeon (ergot has been suggested). None is proven.

## Dionysus: the wild god

The rites of Dionysus took his worshippers, mostly women, out of the city and onto the mountain, where they danced by torchlight in fawnskins, carrying the θύρσος, a fennel stalk tipped with ivy. How much of what myth and tragedy tell about them happened in real life is disputed. [Euripides](author:tlg0006)' *Bacchae* shows the god's power at its most terrifying. A herdsman describes how the maenads of Thebes, disturbed on the mountain, fell on the grazing cattle with their bare hands:

> αἳ δὲ νεμομέναις χλόην μόσχοις ἐπῆλθον χειρὸς ἀσιδήρου μέτα. καὶ τὴν μὲν ἂν προσεῖδες εὔθηλον πόριν μυκωμένην ἔχουσαν ἐν χεροῖν δίχα, ἄλλαι δὲ δαμάλας διεφόρουν σπαράγμασιν.
> "They went for the grazing cattle, with no iron in their hands. You would have seen one holding a young heifer with swollen udders, bellowing, torn in two between her hands, while others ripped apart grown cows."
> ([Euripides, *Bacchae* 734–738](read:tlg0006.tlg017:735))

By the end of the play they do the same to the king of Thebes, and his mother carries his head home thinking it is a lion's.

A third kind of mystery was linked to Orpheus, the mythical singer. Its followers avoided meat and believed the soul could escape a cycle of rebirths. Some were buried with small gold leaves bearing instructions for the soul in the underworld: which spring to avoid, which to drink from, and what to say to the guards ("I am a child of Earth and starry Sky"). These tablets have been found in graves from southern Italy to Crete; they are not in this library's texts.

## Delphi: ask the god

The most famous oracle was Apollo's at Delphi. A consultant paid a fee, sacrificed a goat, and put his question; a priestess, the Pythia, seated on a tripod in the inner room, gave the answer in the god's name, and priests may have put it into verse. Ancient writers speak of a vapour or breath rising from the earth. Whether there was one is still argued: geologists have found faults and traces of gases under the temple, but not everyone is convinced.

Most questions were ordinary (should I marry? should we found a colony?), but [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) remembers the grand ones. King Croesus of Lydia tested the oracles of the world by asking what he was doing on a given day. Delphi alone got it right:

> ὀδμή μʼ ἐς φρένας ἦλθε κραταιρίνοιο χελώνης ἑψομένης ἐν χαλκῷ ἅμʼ ἀρνείοισι κρέεσσιν, ᾗ χαλκὸς μὲν ὑπέστρωται, χαλκὸν δʼ ἐπιέσται.
> "The smell has reached my senses of a hard-shelled tortoise boiling in bronze with the flesh of a lamb: bronze lies beneath it, and bronze is laid on top."
> ([Herodotus 1.47.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:1.47.3))

He had indeed chopped up a tortoise and a lamb and boiled them in a bronze pot with a bronze lid. Convinced, he asked whether to attack Persia, and was told that if he did

> μεγάλην ἀρχὴν μιν καταλύσειν
> "he would destroy a great empire."
> ([Herodotus 1.53.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:1.53.3))

He did: his own. When he complained, the god's answer, as Herodotus tells it, was that he should have asked which empire.

In 480 BCE, with the Persian army approaching, the Athenians were told that Zeus would grant them a "wooden wall" that alone would not be taken:

> τεῖχος Τριτογενεῖ ξύλινον διδοῖ εὐρύοπα Ζεύς μοῦνον ἀπόρθητον τελέθειν, τὸ σὲ τέκνα τʼ ὀνήσει.
> "Far-seeing Zeus grants Athena a wooden wall, alone not to be taken, which will help you and your children."
> ([Herodotus 7.141.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:7.141.3))

Some thought it meant the old thorn hedge round the Acropolis. Themistocles argued it meant the ships, and the fleet won at Salamis. Historians suspect that some oracles were improved after the event.

## "No one is wiser"

The oracle's most famous answer to a private question concerned a man who owned almost nothing. Socrates' friend Chaerephon went to Delphi and asked whether anyone was wiser than Socrates:

> ἤρετο γὰρ δὴ εἴ τις ἐμοῦ εἴη σοφώτερος. ἀνεῖλεν οὖν ἡ Πυθία μηδένα σοφώτερον εἶναι.
> "He asked whether anyone was wiser than I. And the Pythia replied that no one was wiser."
> ([Plato, *Apology* 21a](read:tlg0059.tlg002:21a))

Puzzled, Socrates set out to prove the god wrong by questioning people with a reputation for wisdom, and concluded that he was wiser only in knowing that he knew nothing. He made many enemies doing it.

Delphi was not the only oracle. At Dodona in northern Greece people scratched their questions on thin strips of lead, thousands of which survive: whether a trip will go well, whether to take up sheep-farming, whether a child is really the asker's own.
