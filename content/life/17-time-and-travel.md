# Telling time and travel

Greeks told the hour by the length of their shadow, timed speeches in court with a water clock, and lived by a calendar that each city ran for itself, and that the gods, according to one comedy, could not keep up with. Travel meant walking, riding a mule or taking a ship in the sailing season, and news moved at the speed of a runner.

---

## Shadows and water

There were no clocks on the wall. The day ran from dawn to dusk and was divided, when it was divided at all, into twelve hours that stretched in summer and shrank in winter. Most people went by the sun: the market filled in the morning, the heat of noon was for resting, and dinner came when your shadow was long. In [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019)' *Assemblywomen*, the new women's government promises the men that they will have nothing to do but eat:

> σοὶ δὲ μελήσει, ὅταν ᾖ δεκάπουν τὸ στοιχεῖον, λιπαρὸν χωρεῖν ἐπὶ δεῖπνον.
> "Your only concern will be, when your shadow is ten feet long, to go to dinner all oiled and sleek."
> ([Aristophanes, *Assemblywomen* 651–652](read:tlg0019.tlg010:651))

Sundials came into use from the fifth century. For measured time there was the κλεψύδρα, the "water-thief": a pot with a hole near the bottom, which emptied in a set time. Athenian courts used them to time the speeches. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086)'s account of the courts explains:

> εἰσὶ δὲ κλεψύδραι αὐλίσκους ἔχουσαι ἔκρους, εἰς ἃς τὸ ὕδωρ ἐγχέουσι, πρὸς ὃ δεῖ λέγειν περὶ τὰς δίκας.
> "There are water clocks with small outlet pipes, into which they pour the water by which the speeches in the trials are timed."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 67.2](read:tlg0086.tlg003:67.2))

In a big case each side got ten jugs of water for the first speech. When a speaker had a law or a witness read out, he would tell the attendant to stop the water, so as not to lose time.

## Every city its own calendar

The Greek year followed the moon, twelve months of twenty-nine or thirty days, with an extra month slipped in every few years to keep in step with the seasons. Each city named its own months and started its year at a different time. Athens counted years by the name of the archon in office; Sparta by its chief magistrate; historians eventually used the Olympic games. Athens even ran two calendars at once, one by the moon for festivals and one for the council, and the officials in charge of the festival calendar sometimes added or dropped days for convenience. In *Clouds*, the Moon sends the audience a complaint:

> ὑμᾶς δʼ οὐκ ἄγειν τὰς ἡμέρας οὐδὲν ὀρθῶς, ἀλλʼ ἄνω τε καὶ κάτω κυδοιδοπᾶν· ὥστʼ ἀπειλεῖν φησιν αὐτῇ τοὺς θεοὺς ἑκάστοτε ἡνίκʼ ἂν ψευσθῶσι δείπνου κἀπίωσιν οἴκαδε, τῆς ἑορτῆς μὴ τυχόντες κατὰ λόγον τῶν ἡμερῶν.
> "You don't keep the days properly at all, but jumble them up and down, so that the gods threaten her every time they are cheated of a dinner and go home without the festival they expected by the proper count of days."
> ([Aristophanes, *Clouds* 615–619](read:tlg0019.tlg003:615))

Farmers kept a simpler calendar by the stars. [Hesiod](author:tlg0020) tells his brother when to reap and when to plough:

> πληιάδων Ἀτλαγενέων ἐπιτελλομενάων ἄρχεσθʼ ἀμήτου, ἀρότοιο δὲ δυσομενάων.
> "When the Pleiades, daughters of Atlas, are rising, begin the harvest; when they are setting, the ploughing."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 383–384](read:tlg0020.tlg002:383))

The Pleiades rise at dawn in May and set at dawn in November.

## Roads and runners

Most people walked. Roads were tracks, paved only near sanctuaries, with ruts cut in the rock for cart wheels; inns were few and unpleasant, and travellers relied on friends abroad, the ξένοι bound to them by guest-friendship. News travelled by runner. Before the battle of Marathon in 490 BCE, [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) says, the Athenians sent a professional courier, Pheidippides, to ask Sparta for help:

> δευτεραῖος ἐκ τοῦ Ἀθηναίων ἄστεος ἦν ἐν Σπάρτῃ
> "On the day after he left the city of Athens, he was in Sparta."
> ([Herodotus 6.106.1](read:tlg0016.tlg001:6.106.1))

That is about 240 kilometres in under two days, a feat modern ultra-runners have repeated. The famous run from Marathon to Athens, with the runner gasping "We have won!" and dropping dead, is much later. The earliest surviving versions are in Plutarch and in [Lucian](author:tlg0062), six centuries after the battle:

> πρῶτος δʼ αὐτὸ Φιλιππίδης ὁ ἡμεροδρομήσας λέγεται ἀπὸ Μαραθῶνος ἀγγέλλων τὴν νίκην εἰπεῖν πρὸς τοὺς ἄρχοντας … Χαίρετε, νικῶμεν, καὶ τοῦτο εἰπὼν συναποθανεῖν τῇ ἀγγελίᾳ
> "The first to use it, they say, was Philippides the long-distance runner, bringing the news of the victory from Marathon to the magistrates: 'Joy to you, we have won!', and with those words he died along with his news."
> ([Lucian, *A Slip of the Tongue in Greeting* 3](read:tlg0062.tlg058:3))

Lucian is explaining the origin of the greeting χαίρετε. Herodotus, who describes Marathon in detail, never mentions the run. The modern marathon race was invented for the first modern Olympics, in 1896.

The Persians did better with relays. Herodotus admired their royal couriers, a horse and rider posted at every day's journey:

> τοὺς οὔτε νιφετός, οὐκ ὄμβρος, οὐ καῦμα, οὐ νὺξ ἔργει μὴ οὐ κατανύσαι τὸν προκείμενον αὐτῷ δρόμον τὴν ταχίστην.
> "Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor night keeps them from finishing their appointed stage as fast as they can."
> ([Herodotus 8.98.1](read:tlg0016.tlg001:8.98.1))

The words are carved on the main post office in New York.

## Secret messages

Messages could be intercepted, so people got inventive. When the tyrant Histiaeus, held at the Persian court, wanted to tell his son-in-law to revolt, he

> τῶν δούλων τὸν πιστότατον ἀποξυρήσας τὴν κεφαλὴν ἔστιξε καὶ ἀνέμεινε ἀναφῦναι τὰς τρίχας
> "shaved the head of his most trusted slave, tattooed it, and waited for the hair to grow back."
> ([Herodotus 5.35.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:5.35.3))

The slave's only instruction was to ask the recipient to shave his head.

## The sailing season

Ships were the fastest way to travel, and the most dangerous. Sailing stopped in winter. [Hesiod](author:tlg0020), who hated the sea and had crossed it only once (a few kilometres, to Euboea), gives the safe window:

> ἤματα πεντήκοντα μετὰ τροπὰς ἠελίοιο … ὡραῖος πέλεται θνητοῖς πλόος
> "For fifty days after the summer solstice … is the right season for mortals to sail."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 663–665](read:tlg0020.tlg002:663))

Ships hugged the coast and put in at night. To avoid sailing round the stormy south of Greece, ships could be hauled across the Isthmus of Corinth on a paved track, the δίολκος, whose grooves can still be seen.
