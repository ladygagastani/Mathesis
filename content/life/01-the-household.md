# The household

The Greek word οἶκος *oikos* meant the house, the family in it and everything they owned, slaves and oxen included. Running one was an art with its own name, οἰκονομία, which is where *economy* comes from.

---

## A farmer's shopping list

The poet [Hesiod](author:tlg0020), a farmer in Boeotia around 700 BCE, tells his brother what to get first:

> οἶκον μὲν πρώτιστα γυναῖκά τε βοῦν τʼ ἀροτῆρα
> "First of all a house, a woman, and an ox for the plough."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 405](read:tlg0020.tlg002:405))

The next line specifies "a woman bought, not married, who can follow the oxen too", so the woman in question is a slave. Some editors think that line was added later, which is itself telling: someone felt the list needed explaining.

## Mud walls and a courtyard

Most houses were built of sun-dried mud brick on a low stone footing, with a tiled or thatched roof. Rooms opened onto a courtyard, which gave light and air and usually held an altar. Streets saw mostly blank walls. At Olynthus in northern Greece, where the city was destroyed in 348 BCE and never rebuilt, whole blocks of such houses have been excavated, each on a plot about seventeen metres square.

Mud brick had one obvious weakness. The ordinary Greek word for a burglar was τοιχωρύχος, "wall-digger": he did not pick the lock, he dug through the wall.

## Upstairs, downstairs

Writers of the time describe a house split into men's rooms (the ἀνδρών, where the men dined and entertained guests) and women's rooms. How strict the division was in practice is debated: archaeologists often cannot tell which room was which. One household we can see clearly, because its owner described it in court. Euphiletus, on trial for killing his wife's lover, explains the layout:

> οἰκίδιον ἔστι μοι διπλοῦν, ἴσα ἔχον τὰ ἄνω τοῖς κάτω κατὰ τὴν γυναικωνῖτιν καὶ κατὰ τὴν ἀνδρωνῖτιν.
> "I have a small house on two floors, the upper the same size as the lower, with the women's rooms and the men's rooms."
> ([Lysias 1.9](read:tlg0540.tlg001:9))

When the baby came, he says, he moved upstairs and the women downstairs, so that his wife would not risk the steep stairs every time the baby needed washing. That arrangement is what made the affair possible. (The whole speech, *On the Murder of Eratosthenes*, reads like a short story.)

## A fourteen-year-old in charge

[Xenophon](author:tlg0032)'s *Economics* is a dialogue about running an estate. The gentleman farmer Ischomachus explains how he trained his wife, who came to him

> ἔτη μὲν οὔπω πεντεκαίδεκα γεγονυῖα … ὅπως ὡς ἐλάχιστα μὲν ὄψοιτο, ἐλάχιστα δʼ ἀκούσοιτο, ἐλάχιστα δʼ ἔροιτο
> "not yet fifteen years old … brought up to see as little as possible, hear as little as possible, and ask as few questions as possible."
> ([Xenophon, *Economics* 7.5](read:tlg0032.tlg003:7.5))

He then gives her the tour: storerooms, the cool rooms for summer and the sunny ones for winter, and the door between the women's and men's quarters, which was kept bolted

> ἵνα μήτε ἐκφέρηται ἔνδοθεν ὅ τι μὴ δεῖ μήτε τεκνοποιῶνται οἱ οἰκέται ἄνευ τῆς ἡμετέρας γνώμης
> "so that nothing is carried out that should not be, and so that the slaves do not have children without our say."
> ([Xenophon, *Economics* 9.5](read:tlg0032.tlg003:9.5))

## Water, fire and light

There was no running water in an ordinary house. Water came from a well in the courtyard or from the public fountain house, carried home in a jar on the head, and fetching it was work for women and slaves. In [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019)' *Lysistrata* the chorus of old women arrives with water jars, complaining of the crush at the fountain at dawn,

> δούλαισιν ὠστιζομένη στιγματίαις θʼ
> "shoved about by slave girls, branded ones too."
> ([Aristophanes, *Lysistrata* 331](read:tlg0019.tlg007:331))

Rooms were heated by portable charcoal braziers and lit by small clay lamps burning olive oil. After dark most people simply went to bed.

## Gods of the house

Every house had its gods. Hestia was the hearth itself. Zeus Herkeios, "of the enclosure", had an altar in the courtyard; Zeus Ktesios, "of possessions", guarded the storeroom, sometimes in the form of a sealed jar. A herm, a pillar with a head and a phallus, often stood by the street door. These were not private matters. At Athens a man chosen for one of the nine top offices was questioned in public first:

> εἰ ἔστιν αὐτῷ Ἀπόλλων Πατρῷος καὶ Ζεὺς Ἑρκεῖος, καὶ ποῦ ταῦτα τὰ ἱερά ἐστιν, εἶτα ἠρία εἰ ἔστιν καὶ ποῦ ταῦτα, ἔπειτα γονέας εἰ εὖ ποιεῖ
> "whether he has an Apollo of his fathers and a Zeus of the courtyard, and where these shrines are; then whether he has family tombs and where they are; then whether he treats his parents well."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 55.3](read:tlg0086.tlg003:55.3))

The household, in other words, was the unit the city was built from. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086) says as much at the start of his *Politics*, where he takes a household apart into its three relationships: master and slave, husband and wife, father and children. The first of those has its own article: [Slavery](02-slavery.md).
