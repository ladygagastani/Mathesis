# Games, pets and jokes

Greeks gambled with sheep's knucklebones, raced cocks and quails, kept dogs, birds and the occasional pet leopard, and told jokes that still work. Their oldest surviving joke book is full of absent-minded professors.

---

## Knucklebones

The ἀστράγαλος, the ankle bone of a sheep or goat, has four long sides of different shapes, and Greeks threw handfuls of them as dice. Each side had a value; the best throw, with all four showing different sides, was called "Aphrodite", the worst "the dog". Children played catching and throwing games with them, adults bet on them, and people consulted them as oracles. Their antiquity is shown by a grim story in the *Iliad*. The ghost of Patroclus reminds Achilles how he first came to his father's house as a boy:

> ἤματι τῷ ὅτε παῖδα κατέκτανον Ἀμφιδάμαντος νήπιος οὐκ ἐθέλων ἀμφʼ ἀστραγάλοισι χολωθείς
> "on the day I killed the son of Amphidamas, a child myself, and not meaning to, in a quarrel over knucklebones."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 23.87–88](read:tlg0012.tlg001:23.87))

He was sent into exile for it (see [Exposure, exile and punishment](19-exposure-exile-punishment.md)). Dice with numbered faces, much like ours, were also used, and board games: πεττεία, a game of capture played with pebbles on a grid, and "Five Lines". A famous vase by Exekias shows Achilles and Ajax bent over a board game in the middle of the war.

## Cocks and quails

Animal fights were popular entertainment. [Aelian](author:tlg0545) says the Athenians held a public cockfight in the theatre once a year, and gives the reason: on his way to fight the Persians, Themistocles saw two cocks fighting and told his army they did it not for country, gods or freedom, only so as not to lose. The story may be invented to explain the custom, but the cockfights were real, and so was the betting.

Quails were kept for fights too, and for a game in which one player tapped a quail on the head and the other won if it did not flinch. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) tells how the young Alcibiades made his entrance into Athenian politics. He came into the Assembly while money was being pledged for the city, pledged some himself, and in the applause

> ἐπιλαθέσθαι τοῦ ὄρτυγος ὃν ἐτύγχανεν ἔχων ἐν τῷ ἱματίῳ· πτοηθέντος οὖν καὶ διαφυγόντος ἔτι μᾶλλον ἐκβοῆσαι τοὺς Ἀθηναίους, πολλοὺς δὲ συνθηρᾶν ἀναστάντας
> "forgot the quail he had with him in his cloak. It took fright and escaped, and the Athenians shouted all the louder, and many jumped up to help catch it."
> ([Plutarch, *Alcibiades* 10.1](read:tlg0007.tlg015:10.1))

A ship's captain caught it and gave it back, and became his friend for life.

## Dogs

The most famous dog in Greek literature is Argos, Odysseus' hunting dog, who has waited twenty years lying on a dung heap outside the palace, old and covered in fleas. He is the only one to recognise his master, disguised as a beggar. Too weak to get up, he wags his tail and drops his ears; Odysseus cannot greet him without giving himself away, and wipes away a tear.

> Ἄργον δʼ αὖ κατὰ μοῖρʼ ἔλαβεν μέλανος θανάτοιο, αὐτίκʼ ἰδόντʼ Ὀδυσῆα ἐεικοστῷ ἐνιαυτῷ.
> "And the fate of black death took Argos, as soon as he had seen Odysseus again, in the twentieth year."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 17.326–327](read:tlg0012.tlg002:17.326))

Dogs guarded flocks and houses and went hunting, and small lapdogs, the "Maltese", were pampered pets; there are gravestones and epitaphs for them. Alcibiades once owned an exceptionally fine dog that had cost him seventy minas, a fortune:

> ἀπέκοψε τὴν οὐρὰν πάγκαλον οὖσαν. ἐπιτιμώντων δὲ τῶν συνήθων … ἐπιγελάσας, γίνεται τοίνυν, εἶπεν, ὃ βούλομαι· βούλομαι γὰρ Ἀθηναίους τοῦτο λαλεῖν, ἵνα μή τι χεῖρον περὶ ἐμοῦ λέγωσι.
> "He cut off its tail, which was very beautiful. When his friends told him off … he laughed and said: 'Then what I want is happening. I want the Athenians to chatter about this, so that they say nothing worse about me.'"
> ([Plutarch, *Alcibiades* 9.1](read:tlg0007.tlg015:9.1))

Other pets appear on vases and gravestones: birds in cages, geese, hares, tortoises, and, for the very rich, a cheetah or leopard on a lead.

## The joke professionals

Athens had comedians before it had comedy clubs. [Athenaeus](author:tlg0008) describes a club of wits who met in the sanctuary of Heracles at Diomeia:

> συνελέγοντο ξʹ ὄντες τὸν ἀριθμὸν καὶ ἐν τῇ πόλει διωνομάζοντο ὡς ‘οἱ ξʹ τοῦτʼ εἶπον’ καὶ ‘ἀπὸ τῶν ξʹ ἔρχομαι.’ … τοσαύτη δʼ αὐτῶν δόξα τῆς ῥᾳθυμίας ἐγένετο ὡς καὶ Φίλιππον ἀκούσαντα τὸν Μακεδόνα πέμψαι αὐτοῖς τάλαντον, ἵνʼ ἐκγραφόμενοι τὰ γελοῖα πέμπωσιν αὐτῷ.
> "They met, sixty of them, and in the city people would say 'The Sixty said this' or 'I've just come from the Sixty.' … They became so famous for their wit that Philip of Macedon, hearing of them, sent them a talent to write down their jokes and send them to him."
> ([Athenaeus 14.3](read:tlg0008.tlg001:14.3))

Professional jesters, γελωτοποιοί, sang for their supper at rich men's dinners; one turns up uninvited in [Xenophon](author:tlg0032)'s [*Symposium*](read:tlg0032.tlg004:1.11) and sulks when no one laughs.

## The Laughter-Lover

The only joke book to survive from antiquity is the Φιλόγελως, "Laughter-Lover", a collection of about 265 jokes compiled perhaps in the fourth century CE. It is not in this library. Its favourite target is the σχολαστικός, the egghead, and some of its jokes are still told:

- An egghead wanted to teach his donkey not to eat, so he stopped feeding it. When the donkey died of hunger he said: "What a loss! Just when it had learned not to eat, it died."
- An egghead meets a friend: "I heard you were dead." "But you can see I'm alive." "Yes, but the man who told me is much more reliable than you."
- A chatty barber asks, "How shall I cut your hair?" "In silence." (This one is older: see [Clothes and washing](09-clothes-and-washing.md).)

Other targets are the people of certain towns, who play the part of the fools in the jokes of every country: in Greece they came from Abdera, Sidon and Cyme.
