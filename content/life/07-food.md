# Food

Most Greeks ate barley, olive oil, figs, beans and wine, with cheese, onions and a little fish. Meat was for festivals. The rich obsessed over fish, the Spartans made a virtue of a famously awful black soup, and one Greek city is said to have given its cooks the world's first patent.

---

## Bread and relish

A Greek meal had two parts: σῖτος, the staple, and ὄψον, whatever you ate with it. The staple was grain: for most people barley, kneaded into a flat cake (μᾶζα) or eaten as porridge; for those who could afford it, wheat bread from a baker. The ὄψον might be olives, cheese, onions, a salted fish, a few vegetables. A man who ate too much relish and not enough bread was an ὀψοφάγος, a "relish-eater", which was an insult.

The poor ate what grew wild. [Hesiod](author:tlg0020) complains that the lords who judged his lawsuit do not know

> ὅσον ἐν μαλάχῃ τε καὶ ἀσφοδέλῳ μέγʼ ὄνειαρ
> "how much good there is in mallow and asphodel."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 41](read:tlg0020.tlg002:41))

Mallow was boiled as greens, and still is in parts of Greece; asphodel roots were roasted in the ashes.

## What the philosophers ate

In the *Republic* Socrates sketches a simple, healthy city whose people eat barley cakes and wheat loaves off reeds, with salt, olives, cheese, onions and greens, and for dessert figs, chickpeas and beans, with myrtle berries and acorns roasted in the fire, "drinking in moderation". His friend Glaucon is not impressed:

> εἰ δὲ ὑῶν πόλιν, ὦ Σώκρατες, ἔφη, κατεσκεύαζες, τί ἂν αὐτὰς ἄλλο ἢ ταῦτα ἐχόρταζες;
> "If you were founding a city of pigs, Socrates, what else would you fatten them on?"
> ([Plato, *Republic* 2.372d](read:tlg0059.tlg030:2.372d))

He wants couches, tables and proper cooking, and the argument about justice starts again from there.

Meat mostly came from sacrifices (see [Gods at home and sacrifice](12-gods-at-home.md)), shared out among the worshippers. Some people refused it altogether. Pythagoras' followers are said to have avoided meat, and five centuries later [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) turned the usual question round:

> ἐγὼ δὲ θαυμάζω καὶ τίνι πάθει καὶ ποίᾳ ψυχῇ ἢ λόγῳ ὁ πρῶτος ἄνθρωπος ἥψατο φόνου στόματι καὶ τεθνηκότος ζῴου χείλεσι προσήψατο σαρκός
> "I wonder with what feeling, what mind or reason, the first man put his mouth to gore and touched his lips to the flesh of a dead animal."
> ([Plutarch, *On Eating Meat* 1](read:tlg0007.tlg131:1))

## A drink for heroes

In the *Iliad*, when old Nestor brings a wounded man back to his hut, a slave woman mixes them a restorative, the κυκεών:

> ἐν τῷ ῥά σφι κύκησε γυνὴ ἐϊκυῖα θεῇσιν οἴνῳ Πραμνείῳ, ἐπὶ δʼ αἴγειον κνῆ τυρὸν κνήστι χαλκείῃ, ἐπὶ δʼ ἄλφιτα λευκὰ πάλυνε
> "In it the woman, like a goddess, mixed them Pramnian wine, grated goat's cheese over it with a bronze grater, and sprinkled white barley meal on top."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 11.639–640](read:tlg0012.tlg001:11.639))

Wine, cheese and barley in one cup: the bronze grater has turned up in warriors' graves. A plainer kykeon, of barley and herbs, was the drink of the initiates at Eleusis.

## Fish, sauce and insects

Fresh fish was the luxury Athenians talked about most. Comic poets mock men who haunt the fish stalls, and the fishmongers who wet their stock to make it look fresh. A Sicilian Greek, Archestratus, wrote a whole poem on where to buy the best fish in the Mediterranean and how (simply) to cook it; its fragments survive in [Athenaeus](author:tlg0008)' vast banquet of quotations, *The Learned Banqueters*.

The Greeks also made γάρος, a sauce of fish fermented in salt: the ancestor of the Roman *garum*, and a cousin of the fish sauce of Southeast Asia. Athenaeus notes that the word is masculine, and quotes a line of Aeschylus as proof:

> καὶ τὸν ἰχθύων γάρον.
> "…and the sauce of fish."
> ([Athenaeus 2.75](read:tlg0008.tlg001:2.75))

And there were cicadas. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086), who had clearly tried them, gives tasting notes:

> καὶ τὸ μὲν πρῶτον ἡδίους οἱ ἄρρενες, μετὰ δὲ τὴν ὀχείαν αἱ θήλειαι· ἔχουσι γὰρ ᾠὰ λευκά.
> "At first the males taste better; after mating, the females, because they are full of white eggs."
> ([Aristotle, *History of Animals* 5.30](read:tlg0086.tlg014:5.30))

## The black broth

The most famous dish in Greece was famous for being horrible. At the Spartan common meals the older men gave their meat to the young and made do with the μέλας ζωμός, a black soup usually said to have been pork cooked in blood and vinegar. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) tells what happened when a foreign king tried it:

> λέγεται δέ τινα τῶν Ποντικῶν βασιλέων ἕνεκα τοῦ ζωμοῦ καὶ πρίασθαι Λακωνικὸν μάγειρον εἶτα γευσάμενον δυσχερᾶναι· καὶ τὸν μάγειρον εἰπεῖν ὦ βασιλεῦ, τοῦτον δεῖ τὸν ζωμὸν ἐν τῷ Εὐρώτᾳ λελουμένους ἐποψᾶσθαι.
> "They say that one of the kings of Pontus bought a Spartan cook just for the broth, then tasted it and was disgusted. And the cook said: 'My king, to enjoy this broth you have to have bathed in the Eurotas first.'"
> ([Plutarch, *Lycurgus* 12.7](read:tlg0007.tlg004:12.7))

The Eurotas is Sparta's river, cold even in summer, where the young men trained. A visitor from Sybaris, the Italian city whose name became a byword for luxury, drew a different lesson:

> εἰκότως ἀνδρειότατοι ἁπάντων εἰσὶ Λακεδαιμόνιοι· ἕλοιτο γὰρ ἄν τις εὖ φρονῶν μυριάκις ἀποθανεῖν ἢ οὕτως εὐτελοῦς διαίτης μεταλαβεῖν.
> "No wonder the Spartans are the bravest people in the world. Any sane man would rather die ten thousand times than share such a miserable diet."
> ([Athenaeus 4.15](read:tlg0008.tlg001:4.15))

## A patent on a recipe

The Sybarites themselves, if the stories are true, took food more seriously than anyone. Athenaeus reports a law of theirs:

> εἰ δέ τις τῶν ὀψοποιῶν ἢ μαγείρων ἴδιον εὕροι βρῶμα καὶ περιττόν, ἐξουσίαν μὴ εἶναι χρήσασθαι τούτῳ ἕτερον πρὸ ἐνιαυτοῦ ἀλλʼ αὐτῷ τῷ εὑρόντι
> "If any caterer or cook invented a dish of his own that was out of the ordinary, no one else was allowed to make it for a year, only the man who invented it."
> ([Athenaeus 12.20](read:tlg0008.tlg001:12.20))

The same law exempted eel-sellers and eel-catchers from tax. It is often called the first patent law. It comes, though, from a historian writing long after Sybaris was destroyed in 510 BCE, and tales of Sybarite luxury grew with each telling.
