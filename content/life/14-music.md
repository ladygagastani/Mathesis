# Music

Greek poetry was sung. Epic was chanted to the lyre, choral odes were danced to the pipes, and tragedy was closer to opera than to a modern play. Greeks believed music shaped character, for better or worse, so much so that philosophers wanted to ban some kinds of it, and Spartan magistrates cut the extra strings off a star musician's lyre.

---

## A tortoise and a god

The lyre was made from the shell of a tortoise, with a sounding board of hide, two arms of horn or wood, and gut strings, usually seven. The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* says the god invented it on the day he was born, when he stepped out of his cave and met a tortoise:

> Ἑρμῆς τοι πρώτιστα χέλυν τεκτήνατʼ ἀοιδόν
> "It was Hermes who first made the tortoise into a singer."
> ([*Homeric Hymn to Hermes* 25](read:tlg0013.tlg004:20))

He scoops out the animal, stretches the strings, strikes up a song, and later trades the instrument to his brother Apollo for a herd of cattle he had stolen. The bigger κιθάρα, a wooden box lyre, was the concert instrument of professionals.

The other great instrument was the αὐλός, usually translated "flute" but really a pair of reed pipes played together, shrill and loud, closer to an oboe or a bagpipe chanter. It accompanied sacrifices, drinking parties, rowers, soldiers marching and the choruses of tragedy.

## Athena throws away the pipes

Not everyone approved of the aulos. A myth said that Athena invented it, saw her puffed-out cheeks reflected in water, and threw it away; the satyr Marsyas picked it up, challenged Apollo to a contest, lost, and was flayed alive. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086) repeats the story with a philosopher's twist:

> φασὶ γὰρ δὴ τὴν Ἀθηνᾶν εὑροῦσαν ἀποβαλεῖν τοὺς αὐλούς. οὐ κακῶς μὲν οὖν ἔχει φάναι καὶ διὰ τὴν ἀσχημοσύνην τοῦ προσώπου τοῦτο ποιῆσαι δυσχεράνασαν τὴν θεόν· οὐ μὴν ἀλλὰ μᾶλλον εἰκὸς ὅτι πρὸς τὴν διάνοιαν οὐθέν ἐστιν ἡ παιδεία τῆς αὐλήσεως
> "They say Athena invented the pipes and then threw them away. It is not a bad story that the goddess did it because she disliked the way they spoiled her face; but more likely it is because learning the pipes does nothing for the mind."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 8.1341b](read:tlg0086.tlg035:8.1341b))

The young Alcibiades, [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) says, refused to learn the instrument for the same reason, and set a fashion:

> αὐλείτωσαν οὖν, ἔφη, Θηβαίων παῖδες· οὐ γὰρ ἴσασι διαλέγεσθαι
> "'Let the sons of Thebes play the pipes,' he said, 'since they don't know how to talk.'"
> ([Plutarch, *Alcibiades* 2.5](read:tlg0007.tlg015:2.5))

The lyre, he pointed out, lets you sing while you play; the pipes gag you.

## Music and the soul

Greek music was built on scales called ἁρμονίαι, "tunings", named after peoples: Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Ionian and others. Each was felt to have a character. In the *Republic* [Plato](author:tlg0059) has Socrates purge the music of his ideal city:

> τίνες οὖν θρηνώδεις ἁρμονίαι; λέγε μοι· σὺ γὰρ μουσικός. μειξολυδιστί, ἔφη, καὶ συντονολυδιστὶ καὶ τοιαῦταί τινες. οὐκοῦν αὗται, ἦν δʼ ἐγώ, ἀφαιρετέαι;
> "'Which tunings are like dirges? Tell me, you are the musician.' 'Mixolydian,' he said, 'and tense Lydian, and some others like them.' 'Then these must go,' I said."
> ([Plato, *Republic* 3.398e](read:tlg0059.tlg030:3.398e))

The soft Ionian and Lydian go too, as fit only for drinking parties. Dorian (brave) and Phrygian (calm) may stay. Later Socrates quotes the music theorist Damon for a warning that sounds modern:

> οὐδαμοῦ γὰρ κινοῦνται μουσικῆς τρόποι ἄνευ πολιτικῶν νόμων τῶν μεγίστων
> "Nowhere are the styles of music changed without changing the most important laws of the city."
> ([Plato, *Republic* 4.424c](read:tlg0059.tlg030:4.424c))

What these tunings actually sounded like is only partly understood. Music was written down only rarely, in letter signs above the words. About sixty fragments of notated music survive, most on papyrus or stone and most of them late. The oldest complete song is carved on a gravestone of the first century CE, the epitaph of Seikilos: "While you live, shine; don't grieve at all. Life is short, and time takes its toll." It can be sung today.

## Too many strings

Musicians were stars, and innovation was a scandal. In Sparta, according to a Plutarch collection, the magistrates fined even the great Terpander and nailed up his lyre for adding a single string, and when the virtuoso Timotheus competed at the Carnea festival,

> εἷς τῶν ἐφόρων μάχαιραν λαβὼν ἠρώτησεν αὐτὸν ἐκ ποτέρου τῶν μερῶν ἀποτέμῃ τὰς πλείους τῶν ἑπτὰ χορδῶν.
> "one of the ephors took a knife and asked him from which end he should cut off the strings beyond seven."
> ([Plutarch, *Spartan Customs* 17](read:tlg0007.tlg082a:17))

The story is told in several versions, and the Terpander part is chronologically awkward, but Timotheus, who boasted of his eleven-stringed lyre, was a real and controversial modernist around 400 BCE.

## Schools and theatres

Music was half of an Athenian boy's education: the κιθαριστής taught him to play the lyre and sing the old poets (see [Childhood and school](04-childhood-and-school.md)). A man who could not take the lyre when it came round at a symposium was thought uneducated. In the theatre, each tragedy and comedy had a chorus of twelve or fifteen (tragedy) or twenty-four (comedy) citizens who sang and danced, accompanied by a piper; the playwright composed both the words and the music. The words survive. The music, almost entirely, does not.
