# Slavery

Greek democracy, philosophy and theatre were built on slave labour. Slaves cooked, carried water, nursed children, worked fields and workshops, dug silver out of the ground and even kept order in the Athenian assembly. Almost no one in antiquity argued that slavery itself should end.

---

## How many?

Nobody knows, and the one ancient figure we have is hard to believe. [Athenaeus](author:tlg0008), writing around 200 CE, quotes a census said to have been taken at Athens under Demetrius of Phalerum (317–307 BCE):

> Ἀθηναίους μὲν δισμυρίους πρὸς τοῖς χιλίοις, μετοίκους δὲ μυρίους, οἰκετῶν δὲ μυριάδας μʹ
> "21,000 Athenians, 10,000 resident foreigners, and 400,000 slaves."
> ([Athenaeus 6.103](read:tlg0008.tlg001:6.103))

Most historians think the slave figure is corrupt or wildly inflated. Modern guesses for Attica in the classical period run from about 60,000 to 100,000 slaves, perhaps a quarter to a third of the population. Even a small farmer might own one or two.

## A tool with a soul

[Aristotle](author:tlg0086) put the master's view with chilling precision:

> ὁ δοῦλος κτῆμά τι ἔμψυχον
> "The slave is a piece of property with a soul."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 1.1253b](read:tlg0086.tlg035:1.1253b.ross_section_4))

He goes on to imagine a world where shuttles weave by themselves and picks play the lyre on their own, like the tripods the god Hephaestus made in Homer, which roll into the gods' assembly unaided. Then, he says, masters would need no slaves. It is one of the earliest daydreams about automation, and it ends with slavery, not with freedom.

## The silver mines

The worst work was underground. The silver mines at Laurion in southern Attica paid for the Athenian fleet that beat the Persians, and they were worked by slaves in galleries often too low to stand up in. Rich men owned mining gangs and hired them out. [Xenophon](author:tlg0032) mentions the general Nicias:

> Νικίας ποτὲ ὁ Νικηράτου ἐκτήσατο ἐν τοῖς ἀργυρείοις χιλίους ἀνθρώπους, οὓς ἐκεῖνος Σωσίᾳ τῷ Θρᾳκὶ ἐξεμίσθωσεν, ἐφʼ ᾧ ὀβολὸν μὲν ἀτελῆ ἑκάστου τῆς ἡμέρας ἀποδιδόναι, τὸν δʼ ἀριθμὸν ἴσους ἀεὶ παρέχειν
> "Nicias son of Niceratus once owned a thousand men in the silver mines. He leased them to Sosias the Thracian for an obol a day each, clear of costs, on condition that Sosias always kept the number up."
> ([Xenophon, *Ways and Means* 4.14](read:tlg0032.tlg011:4.14))

"Kept the number up": when a man died, Sosias replaced him.

## Branded, searched, tortured

Runaways could be branded on the forehead; comedy mentions "branded" slaves in passing, as a familiar sight (see [The household](01-the-household.md)). In Athenian courts a slave's evidence counted only if it was taken under torture, on the theory that a slave would otherwise say what the master wanted. Litigants challenged each other to hand over their slaves for questioning, and usually refused.

When the Spartans occupied a fort in Attica in 413 BCE, the slaves voted with their feet. [Thucydides](author:tlg0003) counts the loss:

> ἀνδραπόδων πλέον ἢ δύο μυριάδες ηὐτομολήκεσαν, καὶ τούτων τὸ πολὺ μέρος χειροτέχναι
> "More than twenty thousand slaves had deserted, most of them skilled workers."
> ([Thucydides 7.27.5](read:tlg0003.tlg001:7.27.5))

## Sparta's helots

Sparta was different, and worse. Its farmland was worked by helots, a whole conquered people held in serfdom, who far outnumbered the Spartans. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) describes the *krypteia*, "secret service", in which young Spartans were sent into the countryside with daggers:

> νύκτωρ δὲ κατιόντες εἰς τὰς ὁδοὺς τῶν εἱλώτων τὸν ἁλισκόμενον ἀπέσφαττον
> "At night they came down onto the roads and killed any helot they caught."
> ([Plutarch, *Lycurgus* 28.2](read:tlg0007.tlg004:28.2))

Plutarch adds, citing Aristotle, that each year the Spartan magistrates formally declared war on the helots, so that killing one would not bring religious pollution. He himself doubted the krypteia went back to the lawgiver Lycurgus.

## Ways out

Freedom was possible. Slaves could buy themselves out; masters freed slaves in their wills; at Delphi hundreds of inscriptions record freedom as a "sale" of the slave to Apollo. Some freed slaves did spectacularly well. Pasion, a slave in an Athenian bank, came to own it and was made a citizen. The philosopher [Epictetus](author:tlg0557) was born a slave. So, tradition says, was Aesop, the teller of fables. [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) names his owner:

> δούλη δὲ ἦν Ἰάδμονος τοῦ Ἡφαιστοπόλιος ἀνδρὸς Σαμίου, σύνδουλος δὲ Αἰσώπου τοῦ λογοποιοῦ
> "She was the slave of Iadmon son of Hephaestopolis, a Samian, and a fellow slave of Aesop the storyteller."
> ([Herodotus 2.134.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:2.134.3))

"She" is Rhodopis, later a famous courtesan in Egypt, whose freedom was bought by the brother of the poet Sappho. Herodotus tells the story to correct people who thought she built a pyramid.
