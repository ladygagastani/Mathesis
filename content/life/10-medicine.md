# Medicine

A sick Greek had two kinds of help. One was the god Asclepius, whose temples were part hospital and part pilgrimage site, where patients slept in the hope of a healing dream. The other was the doctor, trained in the tradition that bears the name of Hippocrates, who looked for causes in the body and the weather rather than in the gods. Many people tried both.

---

## Four humours

The writings gathered under the name of [Hippocrates](author:tlg0627) of Cos (around 460–370 BCE) are really a library by many authors. The most influential idea in them is that health is a balance of fluids. The treatise *On the Nature of Man*, probably by Hippocrates' son-in-law Polybus, states it:

> Τὸ δὲ σῶμα τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἔχει ἐν ἑωυτῷ αἷμα καὶ φλέγμα καὶ χολὴν ξανθήν τε καὶ μέλαιναν, καὶ ταῦτʼ ἐστὶν αὐτέῳ ἡ φύσις τοῦ σώματος, καὶ διὰ ταῦτα ἀλγέει καὶ ὑγιαίνει.
> "The human body contains blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. These make up the nature of the body, and through them it feels pain or enjoys health."
> ([Hippocrates, *On the Nature of Man* 4](read:tlg0627.tlg019:4))

Too much or too little of any of them caused illness, and the doctor's job was to restore the balance by diet, exercise, bathing, purges and bleeding. Each humour was linked to a season, and later to a temperament. We still call people *sanguine* (blood), *phlegmatic*, *choleric* (yellow bile) and *melancholic* (black bile, μέλαινα χολή). The theory was wrong, but it ruled European medicine for two thousand years.

## "Life is short"

The most famous sentence in Greek medicine opens the *Aphorisms*:

> ὁ βίος βραχὺς, ἡ δὲ τέχνη μακρὴ, ὁ δὲ καιρὸς ὀξὺς, ἡ δὲ πεῖρα σφαλερὴ, ἡ δὲ κρίσις χαλεπή.
> "Life is short, the art long, opportunity fleeting, experiment treacherous, judgement difficult."
> ([Hippocrates, *Aphorisms* 1.1](read:tlg0627.tlg012:1.1))

"The art" is medicine, and the sentence is about how long it takes to learn it. The Latin *ars longa, vita brevis* turned it round and made it about art.

## Not a sacred disease

Epilepsy was called "the sacred disease", and wandering healers treated it with charms and purifications. The author of the Hippocratic treatise named after it was having none of it:

> περὶ μὲν τῆς ἱερῆς νούσου καλεομένης ὧδʼ ἔχει· οὐδέν τί μοι δοκέει τῶν ἄλλων θειοτέρη εἶναι νούσων οὐδὲ ἱερωτέρη, ἀλλὰ φύσιν μὲν ἔχει ἣν καὶ τὰ λοιπὰ νουσήματα, ὅθεν γίνεται.
> "This is how it is with the so-called sacred disease. It seems to me no more divine and no more sacred than other diseases: like them, it has a natural cause from which it arises."
> ([Hippocrates, *On the Sacred Disease* 1](read:tlg0627.tlg027:1))

He puts the cause in the brain. Other Hippocratic works record case histories day by day, with the patient's name and street, and do not hide how many patients died.

## The Oath

The Hippocratic Oath is sworn by Apollo the Physician, Asclepius, Health and Panacea. The doctor promises to treat his teacher like a parent and to teach the art only to his sons, his teacher's sons and sworn pupils. Then:

> οὐ δώσω δὲ οὐδὲ φάρμακον οὐδενὶ αἰτηθεὶς θανάσιμον … ὁμοίως δὲ οὐδὲ γυναικὶ πεσσὸν φθόριον δώσω. … οὐ τεμέω δὲ οὐδὲ μὴν λιθιῶντας, ἐκχωρήσω δὲ ἐργάτῃσιν ἀνδράσι πρήξιος τῆσδε.
> "I will not give a deadly drug to anyone who asks for it … nor will I give a woman a pessary to cause an abortion. … I will not cut, not even those suffering from stones, but will leave that to men who practise the craft."
> ([Hippocrates, *Oath*](read:tlg0627.tlg013))

Who wrote the Oath, and how many doctors actually swore it, is unknown; it may have belonged to one school. Its ban on cutting is a reminder that surgery was a separate trade.

## Doctors for hire

There were no licences and no medical schools, only apprenticeship and reputation. Cities hired public physicians and paid them from taxes. [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) tells the career of Democedes of Croton, the best doctor of his day, who set up on the island of Aegina with no equipment and outdid the local doctors in his first year:

> καί μιν δευτέρῳ ἔτεϊ ταλάντου Αἰγινῆται δημοσίῃ μισθοῦνται, τρίτῳ δὲ ἔτεϊ Ἀθηναῖοι ἑκατὸν μνέων, τετάρτῳ δὲ ἔτεϊ Πολυκράτης δυῶν ταλάντων.
> "In his second year the Aeginetans hired him at public expense for a talent, in the third the Athenians for a hundred minas, and in the fourth Polycrates for two talents."
> ([Herodotus 3.131.2](read:tlg0016.tlg001:3.131.2))

Captured by the Persians, he found himself among the slaves when King Darius twisted his ankle dismounting from a horse and his Egyptian doctors made it worse. Democedes cured him, was showered with gold by the royal wives, and spent the rest of the story trying to get home.

Childbirth was mostly in women's hands. Socrates' mother was a midwife (see [Women's lives](03-womens-lives.md)), and midwives, not doctors, attended most births.

## Sleeping with the god

The sick who could not afford a doctor, or whom doctors had failed, went to Asclepius. At his sanctuaries at Epidaurus, Cos, Athens and elsewhere, patients bathed, sacrificed and slept in a hall, hoping the god would appear in a dream and cure them or prescribe a treatment. Stone tablets at Epidaurus list the miracles: a man with a spear point in his jaw for six years, a woman pregnant for five years whose son, born in the sanctuary, at once washed himself in the fountain and walked about with his mother. Grateful patients dedicated clay models of the body parts that were healed: ears, eyes, legs, breasts.

[Aristophanes](author:tlg0019) sends the blind god Wealth to be cured at the sanctuary in the Piraeus, and the slave Carion describes the night. The god wiped the patient's eyelids and covered his head with a red cloth:

> εἶθʼ ὁ θεὸς ἐπόππυσεν. ἐξῃξάτην οὖν δύο δράκοντʼ ἐκ τοῦ νεὼ ὑπερφυεῖς τὸ μέγεθος.
> "Then the god whistled, and two enormous snakes darted out of the temple."
> ([Aristophanes, *Wealth* 732–733](read:tlg0019.tlg011:732))

The snakes lick the eyelids, and Wealth can see. Carion, meanwhile, has crept across the hall to eat a pot of porridge left as an offering, after watching the priest collect the others' offerings into a bag for himself.
