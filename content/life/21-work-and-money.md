# Work and money

Most Greeks worked the land, and the rest made things, sold things, rowed the fleet or served as soldiers. Gentlemen looked down on craftsmen who worked with their hands, yet Athens paid its citizens for sitting on juries, and a disabled cobbler could claim a state pension. Coins were a recent invention, and people carried their small change in their mouths.

---

## Work is no disgrace

For [Hesiod](author:tlg0020), a farmer writing to his idle brother around 700 BCE, work was what the gods demanded of mortals:

> ἔργον δʼ οὐδὲν ὄνειδος, ἀεργίη δέ τʼ ὄνειδος.
> "Work is no disgrace; idleness is the disgrace."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 311](read:tlg0020.tlg002:311))

His poem is a farmer's year: when to plough (when you hear the crane calling overhead), when to reap (when the Pleiades rise), when to prune, when to sail, which days of the month are lucky. Nine Greeks in ten lived like this, on small farms growing barley, olives, vines and figs, with a few sheep and goats, and perhaps a slave or two (see [Slavery](02-slavery.md)).

## Hands and bodies

The rich saw it differently. In [Xenophon](author:tlg0032)'s *Economics*, Socrates advises a young landowner to take up farming, the one respectable way to make money, and avoid the trades:

> καὶ γὰρ αἵ γε βαναυσικαὶ καλούμεναι καὶ ἐπίρρητοί εἰσι καὶ εἰκότως μέντοι πάνυ ἀδοξοῦνται πρὸς τῶν πόλεων. καταλυμαίνονται γὰρ τὰ σώματα τῶν τε ἐργαζομένων καὶ τῶν ἐπιμελομένων, ἀναγκάζουσαι καθῆσθαι καὶ σκιατραφεῖσθαι, ἔνιαι δὲ καὶ πρὸς πῦρ ἡμερεύειν.
> "The so-called 'mechanical' crafts are spoken against, and quite rightly held in low esteem by the cities. They ruin the bodies of those who work at them and those in charge of them, forcing them to sit indoors in the shade, and some to spend the whole day by a fire."
> ([Xenophon, *Economics* 4.2](read:tlg0032.tlg003:4.2))

In some cities, he adds, citizens were not allowed to practise a craft at all. Yet the potters, smiths, masons, cobblers and weavers of Athens were citizens, resident foreigners and slaves working side by side; the accounts for the building of the Erechtheum on the Acropolis, carved in stone, show all three working side by side, often at the same rate: a drachma a day. Potters signed their best vases, "Exekias made me".

## Putting the family to work

Poverty could turn the gentleman's rules upside down. [Xenophon](author:tlg0032) remembers Socrates finding his friend Aristarchus miserable: in the civil war of 404 BCE fourteen female relatives had taken refuge in his house, and he could not feed them. Socrates asked what they knew how to do. Spinning, weaving, making clothes, baking. Then let them do it, he said, and sell what they make. Aristarchus borrowed money, bought wool, and

> καὶ ἐργαζόμεναι μὲν ἠρίστων, ἐργασάμεναι δὲ ἐδείπνουν, ἱλαραὶ δὲ ἀντὶ σκυθρωπῶν ἦσαν … τέλος δὲ ἐλθὼν πρὸς τὸν Σωκράτην χαίρων διηγεῖτο ταῦτά τε καὶ ὅτι αἰτιῶνται αὐτὸν μόνον τῶν ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ ἀργὸν ἐσθίειν.
> "they had their lunch while they worked and their dinner when the work was done, and they were cheerful instead of gloomy … In the end he came to Socrates delighted and told him all this, and also that they complained he was the only one in the house who ate without working."
> ([Xenophon, *Memorabilia* 2.7.12](read:tlg0032.tlg002:2.7.12))

## Paid to be a citizen

Athenian democracy paid its citizens to take part, so that the poor could afford to. Pericles introduced pay for jurors in the fifth century, and later Athens paid for attending the Assembly too. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086)'s account lists the rates in his day:

> μισθοφοροῦσι δὲ πρῶτον ὁ δῆμος ταῖς μὲν ἄλλαις ἐκκλησίαις δραχμήν, τῇ δὲ κυρίᾳ ἐννέα ὀβολούς· ἔπειτα τὰ δικαστήρια τρεῖς ὀβολούς· εἶθʼ ἡ βουλὴ πέντε ὀβολούς
> "First, the people are paid a drachma for ordinary meetings of the Assembly and nine obols for the main meeting; then the jury courts three obols; then the Council five obols."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 62.2](read:tlg0086.tlg003:62.2))

Six obols made a drachma, which in the late fifth century was a day's wage for a skilled workman, so jury pay (three obols) was half that: enough to keep an old man going, not to live well. Comedy said the juries were packed with pensioners who came for the three obols.

The same democracy looked after some of those who could not work. A speech of [Lysias](author:tlg0540) was written for a disabled man defending his allowance of one obol a day before the Council, against a neighbour who claimed he was fit and well off:

> τέχνην δὲ κέκτημαι βραχέα δυναμένην ὠφελεῖν, ἣν αὐτὸς μὲν ἤδη χαλεπῶς ἐργάζομαι, τὸν διαδεξόμενον δʼ αὐτὴν οὔπω δύναμαι κτήσασθαι.
> "I have a trade that brings in little, which I can now barely work at myself, and I cannot yet afford to buy someone to take it over from me."
> ([Lysias 24.6](read:tlg0540.tlg024:6))

"Someone to take it over" means a slave. The speech is funny and sharp, and it is one of the few places where an ordinary working man speaks, even if Lysias wrote the words.

## Coins in the mouth

Coins were a recent invention. [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) credits the kingdom of Lydia, in what is now Turkey, around 600 BCE:

> πρῶτοι δὲ ἀνθρώπων τῶν ἡμεῖς ἴδμεν νόμισμα χρυσοῦ καὶ ἀργύρου κοψάμενοι ἐχρήσαντο, πρῶτοι δὲ καὶ κάπηλοι ἐγένοντο.
> "They were the first people we know of to strike and use coins of gold and silver, and the first to become shopkeepers."
> ([Herodotus 1.94.1](read:tlg0016.tlg001:1.94.1))

Athens struck its famous silver "owls", with Athena's head on one side and her owl on the other, from the silver of Laurion (see [Slavery](02-slavery.md)). When a rich new seam was struck in 483 BCE, the Athenians were about to share the money out, ten drachmas each, until Themistocles persuaded them to build two hundred warships instead ([Herodotus 7.144.1](read:tlg0016.tlg001:7.144.1); Aristotle says a hundred). Three years later those ships won the battle of Salamis.

Small coins were tiny, an obol weighing under a gram, and people without pockets carried them in their cheeks. In [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019)' *Wasps* an old juror complains that a friend who was sharing his change at the fish market played a trick on him:

> κἄπειτʼ ἐνέθηκε τρεῖς λοπίδας μοι κεστρέων· κἀγὼ ʼνέκαψʼ· ὀβολοὺς γὰρ ᾠόμην λαβεῖν· κᾆτα βδελυχθεὶς ὀσφρόμενος ἐξέπτυσα
> "Then he put three mullet scales into my hand, and I popped them into my mouth, thinking they were obols; then I smelled them, felt sick, and spat them out."
> ([Aristophanes, *Wasps* 790–792](read:tlg0019.tlg004:790))

## Keeping the market honest

The agora was the city's market as well as its meeting place, with stalls grouped by trade: the fish market, the perfume market, the booksellers. Athens chose officials by lot to police it. Besides the inspectors of goods and the inspectors of weights and measures, there were grain guardians:

> οὗτοι δʼ ἐπιμελοῦνται, πρῶτον μὲν ὅπως ὁ ἐν ἀγορᾷ σῖτος ἀργὸς ὤνιος ἔσται δικαίως, ἔπειθʼ ὅπως οἵ τε μυλωθροὶ πρὸς τὰς τιμὰς τῶν κριθῶν τὰ ἄλφιτα πωλήσουσιν
> "They see to it, first, that unground grain in the market is sold at a fair price, and then that the millers sell barley meal at a price that matches the price of barley."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 51.3](read:tlg0086.tlg003:51.3))

The bakers' loaves had to be of the weight fixed for their price. Athens depended on grain shipped from the Black Sea, and any shortage meant riots, so the grain trade was the most closely watched business in the city. Bankers, τραπεζῖται, "table-men", sat at tables in the market changing money and taking deposits; the richest of them, Pasion, began as a slave in a bank.
