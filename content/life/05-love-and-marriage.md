# Love and marriage

A Greek marriage was arranged between two men, the bride's father and the groom, and sealed with a dowry. The wedding itself was a night of torches, songs and a quince. Love, when the poets speak of it, is more often a sickness than a reason to marry.

---

## The handshake

The legal heart of an Athenian marriage was the ἐγγύη, the "pledge", a promise made by the bride's father or guardian to the groom, often years before the wedding, and often without the bride present. With her came a dowry, usually money or land. It stayed attached to her: if the marriage ended, the husband had to give it back. That made divorce expensive and gave wives some quiet leverage.

The age gap was large, typically a man of about thirty and a girl of fourteen or so ([Women's lives](03-womens-lives.md) quotes [Hesiod](author:tlg0020)'s advice). Before her wedding a girl dedicated her childhood to Artemis. One such offering survives as a poem:

> Τιμαρέτα πρὸ γάμοιο τὰ τύμπανα, τὴν τʼ ἐρατεινὴν σφαῖραν, τόν τε κόμας ῥύτορα κεκρύφαλον, τάς τε κόρας, Λιμνᾶτι, κόρᾳ κόρα, ὡς ἐπιεικές, ἄνθετο, καὶ τὰ κορᾶν ἐνδύματʼ, Ἀρτέμιδι.
> "Timareta, before her wedding, has given you her tambourines, her lovely ball, the net that held her hair, and her dolls, Artemis of the Lake, a girl to a girl, as is right; and her girl's clothes too."
> ([*Greek Anthology* 6.280](read:tlg7000.tlg001@perseus-grc6:6.280))

## The wedding

On the day, the bride bathed in water fetched from a special spring; at Athens, [Thucydides](author:tlg0003) says, it came from the old fountain Callirrhoe:

> καὶ νῦν ἔτι ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀρχαίου πρό τε γαμικῶν καὶ ἐς ἄλλα τῶν ἱερῶν νομίζεται τῷ ὕδατι χρῆσθαι
> "Even now, from ancient custom, they use its water before weddings and for other sacred rites."
> ([Thucydides 2.15.5](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.15.5))

After a feast at her father's house, the bride was driven to her new home in a cart at nightfall, veiled, with torches, flutes and a crowd singing the wedding song, the ὑμέναιος. Homer put the scene on the shield of Achilles:

> νύμφας δʼ ἐκ θαλάμων δαΐδων ὕπο λαμπομενάων ἠγίνεον ἀνὰ ἄστυ, πολὺς δʼ ὑμέναιος ὀρώρει
> "They were leading brides from their rooms through the town under blazing torches, and the wedding song rose loud."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 18.492–493](read:tlg0012.tlg001:18.492))

At the new house she was showered with nuts and dried figs, and one old Athenian law, said to go back to Solon, told her what to eat:

> τὸ τὴν νύμφην τῷ νυμφίῳ συγκαθείργνυσθαι μήλου κυδωνίου κατατραγοῦσαν
> "that the bride be shut in with the groom after eating a quince."
> ([Plutarch, *Solon* 20.3](read:tlg0007.tlg007:20.3))

[Plutarch](author:tlg0007), in his [*Advice to Bride and Groom*](read:tlg0007.tlg078:1), thought the point was that what came from her mouth should be sweet from the start. In Boeotia, he adds, brides were crowned with wild asparagus, because the sweetest fruit grows from the prickliest plant. A friend stood guard at the bedroom door, and the next day the gifts arrived.

## A Spartan wedding

Sparta did it differently, and strangely. Brides were grown women, not girls, and the wedding took the form of a pretend kidnap:

> τὴν δὲ ἁρπασθεῖσαν ἡ νυμφεύτρια καλουμένη παραλαβοῦσα, τὴν μὲν κεφαλὴν ἐν χρῷ περιέκειρεν, ἱματίῳ δὲ ἀνδρείῳ καὶ ὑποδήμασιν ἐνσκευάσασα κατέκλινεν ἐπὶ στιβάδα μόνην ἄνευ φωτός.
> "The bridesmaid took the seized girl, shaved her head to the skin, dressed her in a man's cloak and sandals, and laid her on a straw mattress, alone, in the dark."
> ([Plutarch, *Lycurgus* 15.3](read:tlg0007.tlg004:15.3))

The groom slipped in after dinner in the mess, stayed a short while, then went back to sleep in the barracks with the other young men. Plutarch says some couples had children before they had ever seen each other by daylight.

## When it went wrong

A husband could divorce simply by sending his wife back to her father, with the dowry. A wife had to register the separation with a magistrate in person, which is where Hipparete, the wife of the brilliant and faithless Alcibiades, came unstuck:

> ἐπελθὼν ὁ Ἀλκιβιάδης καὶ συναρπάσας αὐτὴν ἀπῆλθε διʼ ἀγορᾶς οἴκαδε κομίζων, μηδενὸς ἐναντιωθῆναι μηδʼ ἀφελέσθαι τολμήσαντος.
> "Alcibiades came up, seized her, and carried her home through the marketplace, and no one dared to stand in his way or take her from him."
> ([Plutarch, *Alcibiades* 8.4](read:tlg0007.tlg015:8.4))

She stayed with him until she died, not long after.

Adultery was a crime against the husband, and the law let a husband who caught a seducer in the act kill him on the spot. That is the defence in [Lysias](author:tlg0540)' speech [*On the Murder of Eratosthenes*](read:tlg0540.tlg001). The alternatives were humiliation: comedy mentions a radish pushed up the offender's backside and his pubic hair singed off with hot ash.

> τί δʼ ἢν ῥαφανιδωθῇ πιθόμενός σοι τέφρᾳ τε τιλθῇ
> "And what if he gets radished for taking your advice, and plucked with hot ash?"
> ([Aristophanes, *Clouds* 1083](read:tlg0019.tlg003:1083))

How often this happened outside comedy, nobody knows.

## Love

Marriage was about children and property, and some Athenians said so out loud. A speaker in a lawsuit of the 340s BCE put it bluntly:

> τὰς μὲν γὰρ ἑταίρας ἡδονῆς ἕνεκʼ ἔχομεν, τὰς δὲ παλλακὰς τῆς καθʼ ἡμέραν θεραπείας τοῦ σώματος, τὰς δὲ γυναῖκας τοῦ παιδοποιεῖσθαι γνησίως καὶ τῶν ἔνδον φύλακα πιστὴν ἔχειν.
> "Courtesans we keep for pleasure, concubines for the daily care of the body, and wives to bear us legitimate children and keep faithful watch over the house."
> ([Pseudo-Demosthenes 59.122](read:tlg0014.tlg059:122))

The speech, *Against Neaira*, was not in fact by Demosthenes, and it is a prosecutor's rhetoric. Tombstones tell another story: husbands and wives shown clasping hands, and epitaphs praising a wife's love. Romantic love between men and boys was a recognised institution among the upper classes, with its own rules, and is the subject of Plato's [*Symposium*](read:tlg0059.tlg011). The love poetry of the Greek Anthology is addressed to women and men alike. One couplet, credited to Plato himself (probably wrongly), is among the most quoted:

> ἀστέρας εἰσαθρεῖς ἀστήρ ἐμός. εἴθε γενοίμην οὐρανός, ὡς πολλοῖς ὄμμασιν εἰς σὲ βλέπω.
> "You look up at the stars, my Star. I wish I were the sky, to look at you with many eyes."
> ([*Greek Anthology* 7.669](read:tlg7000.tlg001@perseus-grc7:7.669))

## For review (not for publication)
- ἐγγύη: often without the bride present; dowry returnable on divorce (e.g. Isaeus 3.8–9, 35–36; Dem. 59.52). Check phrasing "quiet leverage" (Schaps).
- Anth. Pal. 6.280 (anonymous). "Artemis of the Lake" = Limnatis. τάς τε κόρας = her dolls (κόρη "girl, doll").
- Loutrophoros bath and Callirrhoe/Enneakrounos: Thuc. 2.15.5. Fine.
- Nuts and dried figs (katachysmata): scholia to Ar. Wealth 768. Door-keeper (thyroros) is a friend of the groom (Pollux 3.42).
- Conj. Praec. 138d (section 1 in the Perseus file): quince interpretation and the Boeotian asparagus crown.
- Lyc. 15.3–5; the "not seen by daylight" line is 15.5 (ἐνίους καὶ παῖδας γενέσθαι πρὶν ...). Check.
- Alc. 8.3–4 (divorce registration: ἀπόλειψις before the archon).
- Lysias 1.30 cites the law. Radish and ash: Clouds 1083 with scholia; Wealth 168. The frequency point is Carey 1993 ("Return of the radish").
- [Dem.] 59 by Apollodorus; date c. 343–340 BCE.
- Anth. Pal. 7.669 attributed to Plato (DL 3.29); widely doubted. Two-line epigram; our rendering "Star" keeps ἀστήρ as a name (the boy Aster).
