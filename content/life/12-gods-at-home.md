# Gods at home and sacrifice

Greek religion had no holy book, no creed and no church. It was something you did: a pinch of incense at the hearth, a splash of wine before drinking, an animal killed at the altar and shared at a feast, a procession through the streets. The gods were everywhere, and they expected their share.

---

## The gods' share

The central act of worship was θυσία, animal sacrifice. The victim, an ox, sheep, pig or goat, was led to the altar garlanded; barley grains were thrown, water sprinkled on its head so that it seemed to nod its consent; then its throat was cut, and at the moment of the blow the women raised a high shrill cry, the ὀλολυγή. The *Iliad* describes the sequence as a routine the audience knew by heart:

> αὐτὰρ ἐπεί ῥʼ εὔξαντο καὶ οὐλοχύτας προβάλοντο, αὐέρυσαν μὲν πρῶτα καὶ ἔσφαξαν καὶ ἔδειραν, μηρούς τʼ ἐξέταμον κατά τε κνίσῃ ἐκάλυψαν δίπτυχα ποιήσαντες, ἐπʼ αὐτῶν δʼ ὠμοθέτησαν
> "When they had prayed and thrown the barley grains, first they drew back the heads, cut the throats and flayed the animals; they cut out the thigh bones, wrapped them in a double fold of fat, and laid raw meat on top."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 1.458–461](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.458))

The thigh bones burned for the gods, sending up the savour; the people ate the meat. Why the gods got the bones, [Hesiod](author:tlg0020) explains with a story. At the first sacrifice the Titan Prometheus divided an ox into two portions, meat hidden under the stomach and bones hidden under glistening fat, and let Zeus choose. Zeus took the fat, found the bones, and in his anger withheld fire from mankind. Ever since,

> ἐκ τοῦ δʼ ἀθανάτοισιν ἐπὶ χθονὶ φῦλʼ ἀνθρώπων καίουσʼ ὀστέα λευκὰ θυηέντων ἐπὶ βωμῶν.
> "because of that, the tribes of men on earth burn white bones for the immortals on fragrant altars."
> ([Hesiod, *Theogony* 556–557](read:tlg0020.tlg001:556))

A big sacrifice was also a barbecue. For many Greeks the city's festivals were the only time they ate meat (see [Food](07-food.md)).

## Gods of the house and the street

Every house had its shrines: the hearth of Hestia, the courtyard altar of Zeus Herkeios, Zeus Ktesios in the storeroom (see [The household](01-the-household.md)). A small offering went into the fire at meals, and a few drops of unmixed wine were poured for the gods before a symposium began. At the door stood a herm, a square pillar with the head of Hermes and an erect phallus. They were everywhere in Athens, and when almost all of them were mutilated in one night in 415 BCE, on the eve of the great expedition to Sicily, the city panicked. [Thucydides](author:tlg0003) explains:

> ὅσοι Ἑρμαῖ ἦσαν λίθινοι ἐν τῇ πόλει τῇ Ἀθηναίων (εἰσὶ δὲ κατὰ τὸ ἐπιχώριον, ἡ τετράγωνος ἐργασία, πολλοὶ καὶ ἐν ἰδίοις προθύροις καὶ ἐν ἱεροῖς), μιᾷ νυκτὶ οἱ πλεῖστοι περιεκόπησαν τὰ πρόσωπα.
> "The stone herms in the city of Athens (these are the square-cut pillars of local custom, found in great numbers in the doorways of private houses and in temples) were in one night most of them mutilated in the face."
> ([Thucydides 6.27.1](read:tlg0003.tlg001:6.27.1))

Informers named names, men were executed, and the orator [Andocides](author:tlg0027) turned informer to save himself and his relatives; his speech [*On the Mysteries*](read:tlg0027.tlg001) tells his side.

At crossroads stood shrines of Hecate, goddess of ghosts and boundaries. At the new moon, households left a meal there for her. In [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019)' *Wealth*, a man points out what really happened to it:

> φησὶ γὰρ αὕτη τοὺς μὲν ἔχοντας καὶ πλουτοῦντας δεῖπνον κατὰ μῆνʼ ἀποπέμπειν, τοὺς δὲ πένητας τῶν ἀνθρώπων ἁρπάζειν πρὶν καταθεῖναι.
> "She says the rich and well-to-do send her a dinner every month, and the poor snatch it before it has even been set down."
> ([Aristophanes, *Wealth* 596–597](read:tlg0019.tlg011:596))

## A year of festivals

Athens had more festivals than any other Greek city, the Athenians boasted, and its calendar was built round them. The Panathenaea brought a new robe to Athena's statue every year, carried in a procession that the Parthenon frieze shows. At the City Dionysia, in spring, the tragedies and comedies were performed. At the Anthesteria in February the new wine was opened, everyone from the age of three had their own jug for a drinking contest, and the spirits of the dead were believed to roam the city, so people chewed buckthorn and smeared their doors with pitch to keep them out. At the Thesmophoria the women took over (see [Women's lives](03-womens-lives.md)).

Festivals were how the city fed its people and paid for them: the richest citizens were required to fund choruses and feasts, a kind of tax called a *liturgy* (λειτουργία, "work for the people"). The word now means a church service.

## Bargaining with the gods

Prayer was frank. The worshipper reminded the god what he had done for him and said what he wanted in return: "give, because I gave", or "give, and I will give". The priest Chryses opens his prayer in the *Iliad* with exactly that reminder: "if ever I roofed a temple to please you, or burned fat thighs of bulls and goats for you, grant me this wish" ([*Iliad* 1.39–41](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.39)). Vows were public contracts, and sanctuaries filled with the evidence that they had been kept: statues, tripods, weapons taken from enemies, model ships, and clay feet and eyes from the cured (see [Medicine](10-medicine.md)). Nobody thought a god could be ordered about, but nobody thought devotion should go unpaid.
