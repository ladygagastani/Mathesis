# Plague and war

War was the normal state of Greek life: most cities fought a neighbour most years, and every citizen was a soldier. The worst war of all, between Athens and Sparta, brought a plague that killed perhaps a quarter of the people of Athens, and a historian who caught it and lived to describe it.

---

## The plague of Athens

In the summer of 430 BCE, the second year of the Peloponnesian War, the whole population of the Athenian countryside was crowded inside the city walls while the Spartans burned the farms outside. Then a disease arrived by ship, probably from Egypt through the port of Piraeus. [Thucydides](author:tlg0003) says nothing like it had been remembered anywhere:

> οὐ μέντοι τοσοῦτός γε λοιμὸς οὐδὲ φθορὰ οὕτως ἀνθρώπων οὐδαμοῦ ἐμνημονεύετο γενέσθαι.
> "Nowhere was there a memory of so great a plague, or such a destruction of human life."
> ([Thucydides 2.47.3](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.47.3))

He refuses to guess at its cause, and describes it instead, for a reason:

> ἐγὼ δὲ οἷόν τε ἐγίγνετο λέξω, καὶ ἀφʼ ὧν ἄν τις σκοπῶν, εἴ ποτε καὶ αὖθις ἐπιπέσοι, μάλιστʼ ἂν ἔχοι τι προειδὼς μὴ ἀγνοεῖν, ταῦτα δηλώσω αὐτός τε νοσήσας καὶ αὐτὸς ἰδὼν ἄλλους πάσχοντας.
> "I will say what it was like, and set out the signs by which anyone who studies them might recognise it if it ever strikes again; for I had it myself, and saw others suffer from it."
> ([Thucydides 2.48.3](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.48.3))

What follows is the first clinical description of an epidemic in European literature. It began with fever in the head and red, inflamed eyes; the throat and tongue turned bloody, the breath foul; then came sneezing, hoarseness, a cough, vomiting of bile, spasms. The skin broke out in small blisters and ulcers, and the sick burned so much inside that they could not bear even the lightest clothing and threw themselves into cisterns. Those who survived the first week often died of the diarrhoea that followed; some who recovered lost their fingers, toes or eyes, or their memory.

Even scavengers kept away:

> τὰ γὰρ ὄρνεα καὶ τετράποδα ὅσα ἀνθρώπων ἅπτεται, πολλῶν ἀτάφων γιγνομένων ἢ οὐ προσῄει ἢ γευσάμενα διεφθείρετο.
> "The birds and beasts that feed on human flesh, though many bodies lay unburied, either did not come near them or died after tasting them."
> ([Thucydides 2.50.1](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.50.1))

What was it? Typhus, typhoid, smallpox, measles and even an Ebola-like fever have all been proposed. In the 1990s a mass grave of the right date was found in the Kerameikos cemetery, and a DNA study in 2006 claimed to find typhoid fever in the teeth; other scientists questioned the method. The question is open.

## When the laws broke down

Thucydides is most interested in what the plague did to people. Nursing the sick meant catching it, so the dying were abandoned; the dead lay in heaps in the temples; the old funeral customs collapsed:

> ἐπὶ πυρὰς γὰρ ἀλλοτρίας φθάσαντες τοὺς νήσαντας οἱ μὲν ἐπιθέντες τὸν ἑαυτῶν νεκρὸν ὑφῆπτον, οἱ δὲ καιομένου ἄλλου ἐπιβαλόντες ἄνωθεν ὃν φέροιεν ἀπῇσαν.
> "Some would get to a pyre that others had built, put their own dead on it and set it alight; others threw the body they were carrying on top of one already burning, and went away."
> ([Thucydides 2.52.4](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.52.4))

With death so near, people stopped caring about honour or the law: "fear of gods or law of men restrained no one", since nobody expected to live long enough to be punished. The disease came back in 427 and killed, in all, about 4,400 hoplites, 300 cavalry and "a number beyond discovering" of ordinary people. Pericles, the city's leader, died of it in 429.

The old men remembered a prophecy, "A Dorian war will come, and plague with it", and argued over whether the old verse had said λοιμός, "plague", or λιμός, "famine":

> ἐνίκησε δὲ ἐπὶ τοῦ παρόντος εἰκότως λοιμὸν εἰρῆσθαι· οἱ γὰρ ἄνθρωποι πρὸς ἃ ἔπασχον τὴν μνήμην ἐποιοῦντο.
> "For the moment, naturally, 'plague' won: people shaped their memory to what they were suffering."
> ([Thucydides 2.54.3](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.54.3))

## Civil war

The war turned cities against themselves. At Corcyra in 427, democrats and oligarchs massacred each other, fathers killed sons, and men were dragged from the altars. Thucydides' analysis is one of the most quoted passages in Greek:

> τόλμα μὲν γὰρ ἀλόγιστος ἀνδρεία φιλέταιρος ἐνομίσθη, μέλλησις δὲ προμηθὴς δειλία εὐπρεπής, τὸ δὲ σῶφρον τοῦ ἀνάνδρου πρόσχημα
> "Reckless daring was now called loyal courage; careful hesitation, cowardice in disguise; moderation, a cover for weakness."
> ([Thucydides 3.82.4](read:tlg0003.tlg001:3.82.4))

Words, he says, changed their meanings to fit what men were doing.

## Mercy and its absence

Sometimes the verdict of an assembly was all that stood between a city and destruction. In 427 the Athenians voted to kill every man in rebellious Mytilene and enslave the women and children, sent a ship with the order, and changed their minds the next day. A second ship chased the first, a day and a night behind:

> ὥστε ἤσθιόν τε ἅμα ἐλαύνοντες οἴνῳ καὶ ἐλαίῳ ἄλφιτα πεφυραμένα, καὶ οἱ μὲν ὕπνον ᾑροῦντο κατὰ μέρος, οἱ δὲ ἤλαυνον.
> "They ate barley meal mixed with wine and olive oil as they rowed, and slept in turns while the others rowed on."
> ([Thucydides 3.49.3](read:tlg0003.tlg001:3.49.3))

It put in just as the Athenian commander had read the first order and was about to carry it out. Eleven years later the island of Melos, which had wanted to stay neutral, was not so lucky:

> οἱ δὲ ἀπέκτειναν Μηλίων ὅσους ἡβῶντας ἔλαβον, παῖδας δὲ καὶ γυναῖκας ἠνδραπόδισαν
> "They killed all the grown men of Melos they took, and enslaved the children and women."
> ([Thucydides 5.116.4](read:tlg0003.tlg001:5.116.4))

Two years after that, the great Athenian expedition to Sicily was destroyed. The prisoners were packed into the stone quarries of Syracuse, burned by the sun by day and chilled at night, among the piled-up bodies of those who died, on a daily ration of a cup of water and two cups of grain. After seventy days all but the Athenians and the Greeks of Sicily and Italy were sold; the rest stayed in the quarries. "Few out of many came home."

## With your shield or on it

Greek soldiers fought in a close line, each man's round shield covering his own left side and his neighbour's right. To throw away your shield and run was the ultimate disgrace. Spartan mothers, according to a collection of their sayings put together centuries later, sent their sons off with a short instruction:

> τέκνον, ἔφη, ἢ τὰν ἢ ἐπὶ τᾶς.
> "'My son,' she said, 'either this, or on this.'"
> ([Plutarch, *Sayings of Spartan Women* 5.16](read:tlg0007.tlg082b:5.16))

Come back carrying your shield, or be carried home dead on it. Whether any mother said it, we cannot know. But the dead at Thermopylae, where three hundred Spartans held the pass against the Persian army in 480 BCE, really did get an epitaph that became the model of all soldiers' epitaphs:

> ὦ ξεῖνʼ, ἀγγέλλειν Λακεδαιμονίοις ὅτι τῇδε κείμεθα τοῖς κείνων ῥήμασι πειθόμενοι.
> "Stranger, tell the Spartans that we lie here, obedient to their words."
> ([Herodotus 7.228.2](read:tlg0016.tlg001:7.228.2))
