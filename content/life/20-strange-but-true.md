# Strange but true (mostly)

A cabinet of curiosities: a law that made neutrality a crime, a philosopher who lived in a jar, another who would rather die than cross a bean field, a wedding lost to a handstand, and a Byzantine fire weapon whose recipe is still a secret. Some of these are well attested; others are the kind of story the Greeks loved to tell about their famous men. Each one says which.

---

## You must take a side

Most lawgivers try to stop civil war. Solon, according to [Aristotle](author:tlg0086), made it a crime to stay out of one:

> ὁρῶν δὲ τὴν μὲν πόλιν πολλάκις στασιάζουσαν, τῶν δὲ πολιτῶν ἐνίους διὰ τὴν ῥᾳθυμίαν ἀγαπῶντας τὸ αὐτόματον, νόμον ἔθηκεν πρὸς αὐτοὺς ἴδιον, ὃς ἂν στασιαζούσης τῆς πόλεως μὴ θῆται τὰ ὅπλα μηδὲ μεθʼ ἑτέρων, ἄτιμον εἶναι καὶ τῆς πόλεως μὴ μετέχειν.
> "Seeing that the city was often in civil strife, and that some citizens were content out of apathy to let things take their course, he made a special law for them: whoever did not take up arms with one side or the other when the city was divided should lose his rights and have no share in the city."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 8.5](read:tlg0086.tlg003:8.5))

**Attested?** Aristotle and Plutarch both report Solon's law. Some modern historians doubt that Solon made such a law, and how it could have been enforced is anyone's guess. Its point seems to be that the silent majority should not leave the city to the extremists.

Athens had a stranger device still for dealing with its politicians: once a year it could vote a leading citizen into exile for ten years without accusing him of anything, each voter scratching a name on a potsherd. How ostracism worked, and the famous story of the voter who could not write, is told in [Exposure, exile and punishment](19-exposure-exile-punishment.md).

## Diogenes, the dog

Diogenes of Sinope (about 404–323 BCE) was called "the dog", κύων, whence *Cynic*, for living shamelessly in public. The anecdotes collected by [Diogenes Laertius](author:tlg0004) five centuries later are the best-known stories in Greek philosophy. When a friend was slow to find him a small house, he moved into a large storage jar in the Metroön at Athens ([6.2.23](read:tlg0004.tlg001:6.2.23)). He owned a cup until he saw a boy drinking from his hands, and threw it away: "A child has beaten me in plain living" ([6.2.37](read:tlg0004.tlg001:6.2.37)). When Plato defined man as "a featherless animal with two feet", he plucked a chicken and brought it to the Academy:

> οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ Πλάτωνος ἄνθρωπος.
> "Here is Plato's man."
> ([Diogenes Laertius 6.2.40](read:tlg0004.tlg001:6.2.40))

The definition was amended to add "with broad nails". He lit a lamp in daylight and walked about "looking for a human being" ([6.2.41](read:tlg0004.tlg001:6.2.41)). And when Alexander the Great found him sunbathing at Corinth and told him to ask for anything he liked:

> ἀποσκότησόν μου, φησί.
> "'Stand out of my light,' he said."
> ([Diogenes Laertius 6.2.38](read:tlg0004.tlg001:6.2.38))

**Attested?** Diogenes was real and the jar is plausible, but anecdotes gathered around him like iron filings round a magnet. The Alexander meeting is probably invented; it is too good not to be.

## Death before beans

Pythagoras, the mathematician and mystic of the sixth century BCE, taught his followers to abstain from beans, for reasons that were already disputed in antiquity: that beans looked like the gates of Hades, or genitals, or that they were "full of breath" and so of soul. One story of his death makes the rule fatal. Fleeing a mob that had set fire to the house where his followers met,

> πρός τινι χωρίῳ γενόμενος πλήρει κυάμων, ἵνα αὐτόθι ἔστη, εἰπὼν ἁλῶναι ἂν μᾶλλον ἢ πατῆσαι … καὶ ὧδε πρὸς τῶν διωκόντων ἀποσφαγῆναι.
> "he came to a field full of beans and stopped there, saying he would rather be caught than trample them … and so his pursuers cut his throat."
> ([Diogenes Laertius 8.1.39](read:tlg0004.tlg001:8.1.39))

**Attested?** Diogenes Laertius gives several other versions of the death in the same breath, including starvation in a temple. The bean ban is well attested; the bean-field death is legend.

## Philosophers' ends

Ancient biographers loved an ironic death, and Diogenes Laertius collected them:

- **Heraclitus**, who taught that everything is fire and flux, fell ill with dropsy (fluid in the body) and asked the doctors, in a riddle, whether they could make a drought out of a flood. When they did not understand, he buried himself in a cowshed, hoping the warmth of the dung would dry him out, and died ([9.1.3](read:tlg0004.tlg001:9.1.3)). Diogenes also records writers who said he was cured.
- **Empedocles**, who hinted that he was a god, was said to have leapt into the crater of Etna to vanish without trace and prove it, but the volcano threw up one of his bronze sandals ([8.2.69](read:tlg0004.tlg001:8.2.69)). Other versions have him die of a thigh broken in a fall from a carriage.
- **Chrysippus**, the great Stoic logician, saw a donkey eat his figs, told his old servant to give it some neat wine to wash them down, and laughed himself to death ([7.7.185](read:tlg0004.tlg001:7.7.185)). "Some say", Diogenes adds; others say he drank neat wine at a sacrifice and died of dizziness.

## Thales in the well

Thales of Miletus, counted the first Greek philosopher, fell into a well while looking at the stars. [Plato](author:tlg0059) tells it:

> ὥσπερ καὶ Θαλῆν ἀστρονομοῦντα, ὦ Θεόδωρε, καὶ ἄνω βλέποντα, πεσόντα εἰς φρέαρ, Θρᾷττά τις ἐμμελὴς καὶ χαρίεσσα θεραπαινὶς ἀποσκῶψαι λέγεται ὡς τὰ μὲν ἐν οὐρανῷ προθυμοῖτο εἰδέναι, τὰ δʼ ἔμπροσθεν αὐτοῦ καὶ παρὰ πόδας λανθάνοι αὐτόν.
> "Like Thales, Theodorus, who was looking up at the stars and fell into a well, and a witty and charming Thracian slave girl is said to have mocked him for being so eager to know what was in the sky that he could not see what was in front of him and under his feet."
> ([Plato, *Theaetetus* 174a](read:tlg0059.tlg006:174))

[Aristotle](author:tlg0086) has the answer to the jibe. Taunted that philosophy was useless because he was poor, Thales used his astronomy to foresee a bumper olive crop, cheaply hired every olive press in Miletus and Chios in winter, and at harvest time rented them out at his own price, to show that philosophers could easily be rich if they wanted ([*Politics* 1.1259a](read:tlg0086.tlg035:1.1259a)). It may be the first recorded corner in a commodity market. Aristotle himself calls it a story "they tell".

## Dancing away a wedding

Around 575 BCE Cleisthenes, tyrant of Sicyon, held a year-long competition for his daughter's hand. The favourite was the rich and handsome Athenian Hippocleides. On the last night, after dinner, he called for a table, danced on it, and finally stood on his head and waved his legs in the air. Cleisthenes, who had been holding his tongue, could bear it no longer. [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) gives the exchange:

> ὦ παῖ Τισάνδρου, ἀπορχήσαό γε μὲν τὸν γάμον. ὁ δὲ Ἱπποκλείδης ὑπολαβὼν εἶπε οὐ φροντὶς Ἱπποκλείδῃ.
> "'Son of Tisander, you have danced away your marriage.' And Hippocleides replied: 'Hippocleides doesn't care.'"
> ([Herodotus 6.129.4](read:tlg0016.tlg001:6.129.4))

The reply became a proverb. The bride married another Athenian, Megacles, and their great-grandson was Pericles.

## Greek fire

In 672 CE, the story goes, an architect named Callinicus, a refugee from Syria, brought the Byzantine Empire a liquid that burned on water. Pumped through bronze tubes from the bows of warships, it saved Constantinople from Arab fleets more than once, and its recipe was a state secret so well kept that it has been lost. The princess [Anna Comnena](author:tlg2703) describes the fleet her father, Emperor Alexius I, built around 1100 against the Pisans:

> ἐν ἑκάστῃ πρώρᾳ τῶν πλοίων διὰ χαλκῶν καὶ σιδήρων λεόντων καὶ ἀλλοίων χερσαίων ζώων κεφαλὰς μετὰ στομάτων ἀνεῳγμένων κατασκευάσας … τὸ διὰ τῶν στρεπτῶν κατὰ τῶν πολεμίων μέλλον ἀφίεσθαι πῦρ διὰ τῶν στομάτων αὐτῶν παρεσκεύασε διϊέναι
> "On the prow of every ship he set heads of lions and other land animals, in bronze and iron, with open mouths … and arranged that the fire to be launched at the enemy through tubes should pass through their mouths."
> ([Anna Comnena, *Alexiad* 11.10](read:tlg2703.tlg001:11.10))

Fire-breathing lions, gilded to look terrifying. **Attested?** The weapon is real and well documented. What was in it (probably crude oil with resins, delivered under pressure) is still argued over.
