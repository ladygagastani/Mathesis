# Women's lives

Almost everything we know about Greek women was written by men, and most of it by Athenian men of the upper class. Read against the grain, though, the texts show women working, trading, praying, running households and, in Sparta, owning a large share of the land.

---

## "Least talked about"

At Athens a free woman could not vote, hold office or appear in court for herself. Every woman had a κύριος, a guardian: her father, then her husband, then her son. A law quoted in a speech of [Isaeus](author:tlg0017) sets the limit of what she could do on her own:

> ὁ γὰρ νόμος διαρρήδην κωλύει παιδὶ μὴ ἐξεῖναι συμβάλλειν μηδὲ γυναικὶ πέρα μεδίμνου κριθῶν
> "The law expressly forbids a child, or a woman, to make a contract worth more than a bushel of barley."
> ([Isaeus 10.10](read:tlg0017.tlg010:10))

A medimnos of barley fed one person for about six weeks: enough for the shopping, not for a house.

The ideal was invisibility. In the funeral speech that [Thucydides](author:tlg0003) gives to Pericles, the war widows get one sentence of advice:

> μεγάλη ἡ δόξα καὶ ἧς ἂν ἐπʼ ἐλάχιστον ἀρετῆς πέρι ἢ ψόγου ἐν τοῖς ἄρσεσι κλέος ᾖ
> "Great is the glory of the woman who is least talked about among men, for good or for ill."
> ([Thucydides 2.45.2](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.45.2))

## Married at fourteen

Girls married young, men late. [Hesiod](author:tlg0020)'s advice was followed for centuries:

> ὡραῖος δὲ γυναῖκα τεὸν ποτὶ οἶκον ἄγεσθαι, μήτε τριηκόντων ἐτέων μάλα πόλλʼ ἀπολείπων μήτʼ ἐπιθεὶς μάλα πολλά … ἡ δὲ γυνὴ τέτορʼ ἡβώοι, πέμπτῳ δὲ γαμοῖτο.
> "Bring a wife home when you are the right age: not far short of thirty, nor much over … and let the woman be four years past puberty, and marry in the fifth."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 695–698](read:tlg0020.tlg002:695))

A husband of thirty and a wife of fourteen or fifteen: the gap shaped every marriage. (More in [Love and marriage](05-love-and-marriage.md).)

## Three times in the battle line

Childbirth was the most dangerous thing most women ever did. [Euripides](author:tlg0006) gives Medea the most famous protest in Greek literature:

> λέγουσι δʼ ἡμᾶς ὡς ἀκίνδυνον βίον ζῶμεν κατʼ οἴκους, οἳ δὲ μάρνανται δορί· κακῶς φρονοῦντες· ὡς τρὶς ἂν παρʼ ἀσπίδα στῆναι θέλοιμʼ ἂν μᾶλλον ἢ τεκεῖν ἅπαξ.
> "They say we live a life without danger at home, while they fight with the spear. How wrong they are! I would rather stand three times in the battle line than give birth once."
> ([Euripides, *Medea* 248–251](read:tlg0006.tlg003:250))

At Sparta, Plutarch says, the only people allowed a name on their tombstone were men who died in war and women who died in childbirth. Whether that is right depends on how one reads the text, but a handful of such stones have been found.

## Women at work

Poor women could not stay indoors, and did not. In a speech of [Demosthenes](author:tlg0014) a man defending his citizenship admits that his mother sold ribbons in the market and worked as a wet nurse, and says it is nothing to be ashamed of:

> πολλαὶ καὶ τιτθαὶ καὶ ἔριθοι καὶ τρυγήτριαι γεγόνασιν ὑπὸ τῶν τῆς πόλεως κατʼ ἐκείνους τοὺς χρόνους συμφορῶν ἀσταὶ γυναῖκες
> "Many citizen women became wet nurses, wool-workers and grape-pickers because of the city's misfortunes in those days."
> ([Demosthenes 57.45](read:tlg0014.tlg057:45))

Women sold bread, vegetables, garlands and perfume. Midwives were women; Socrates' mother was one. Women wove all the cloth the household wore. Comedy even jokes that Euripides' mother sold wild greens, which may be a slander, but a joke only works if the audience has seen such women in the market.

## Priestesses and festivals

Religion was the one public stage open to women. The priestess of Athena Polias was one of the most important people in Athens, and the Pythia, the voice of Apollo at Delphi, was a woman (see [Mysteries and oracles](13-mysteries-and-oracles.md)). At the Thesmophoria, a festival for Demeter, married women camped out on the Pnyx hill, where the men's assembly usually met, for three days, and no man was allowed near. [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019) built a whole comedy, [*Thesmophoriazusae*](read:tlg0019.tlg008), on a man sneaking in disguised as a woman.

## Sparta's women

Other Greeks found Spartan women shocking. They exercised outdoors, raced and wrestled (Plutarch says the girls stripped for processions and dances like the boys), married later, and could inherit. By [Aristotle](author:tlg0086)'s day they owned a remarkable share of the land:

> ἔστι δὲ καὶ τῶν γυναικῶν σχεδὸν τῆς πάσης χώρας τῶν πέντε μερῶν τὰ δύο
> "Nearly two-fifths of all the land belongs to women."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 2.1270a](read:tlg0086.tlg035:2.1270a))

Asked by a foreign woman why Spartan women were the only ones who ruled their men, Gorgo, the wife of King Leonidas, is said to have answered:

> μόναι γάρ, ἔφη, τίκτομεν ἄνδρας.
> "Because we are the only ones who give birth to men."
> ([Plutarch, *Lycurgus* 14.4](read:tlg0007.tlg004:14.4))

Like most Spartan one-liners, it was collected long after and polished in the telling.

## Women's voices

A few women's own words survive. Sappho of Lesbos (around 600 BCE) was called "the tenth Muse", but her poems come down in scraps and are not in this library. Most of what we have is epitaphs, curse tablets, letters on papyrus from Roman Egypt, and the names women scratched on the things they dedicated to the gods (see [Love and marriage](05-love-and-marriage.md) for one girl's toys).
