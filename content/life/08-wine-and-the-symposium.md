# Wine and the symposium

Greeks drank wine every day, but almost always mixed with water, and drinking it neat was what barbarians did. The high point of a well-off man's social life was the συμπόσιον, "drinking together": an evening of wine, songs, games, talk and sometimes a brawl, with rules about all of them.

---

## Three parts water

Wine was mixed in a large bowl, the κρατήρ, and ladled into cups. How strong to make it was a serious question. [Hesiod](author:tlg0020) recommends a summer drink in the shade:

> τρὶς ὕδατος προχέειν, τὸ δὲ τέτρατον ἱέμεν οἴνου
> "Pour three measures of water, and put in the fourth of wine."
> ([Hesiod, *Works and Days* 596](read:tlg0020.tlg002:596))

Other writers mention two to one, three to two, even one to one for a wild night. Since Greek wine was probably strong and often sweet, three to one gave something like a modern beer.

Drinking wine unmixed, ἄκρατος, was dangerous and foreign. The Spartans explained the madness of their king Cleomenes, who died cutting himself to pieces with a knife, by saying he had picked up the habit from Scythian envoys, and [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) reports their catchphrase:

> ἐπεὰν ζωρότερον βούλωνται πιεῖν, Ἐπισκύθισον λέγουσι.
> "When they want a stronger drink, they say, 'Make it Scythian!'"
> ([Herodotus 6.84.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:6.84.3))

## Ten bowls

A fragment of the comic poet Eubulus, preserved by [Athenaeus](author:tlg0008), has the god Dionysus himself explain how an evening goes:

> τρεῖς γὰρ μόνους κρατῆρας ἐγκεραννύω τοῖς εὖ φρονοῦσι· τὸν μὲν ὑγιείας ἕνα, ὃν πρῶτον ἐκπίνουσι· τὸν δὲ δεύτερον ἔρωτος ἡδονῆς τε· τὸν τρίτον δʼ ὕπνου, ὃν ἐκπιόντες οἱ σοφοὶ κεκλημένοι οἴκαδε βαδίζουσʼ. ὁ δὲ τέταρτος οὐκ ἔτι ἡμέτερός ἐστʼ, ἀλλʼ ὕβρεος· ὁ δὲ πέμπτος βοῆς· ἕκτος δὲ κώμων· ἕβδομος δʼ ὑπωπίων· ὁ δʼ ὄγδοος κλητῆρος· ὁ δʼ ἔνατος χολῆς· δέκατος δὲ μανίας, ὥστε καὶ βάλλειν ποιεῖ.
> "For sensible people I mix only three bowls: the first for health, which they drink first; the second for love and pleasure; the third for sleep, and when they have drunk it, wise guests go home. The fourth bowl is not mine any more, it belongs to violence; the fifth to shouting; the sixth to rampaging through the streets; the seventh to black eyes; the eighth to the summons; the ninth to bile; the tenth to madness, and people throwing things."
> ([Athenaeus 2.3](read:tlg0008.tlg001:2.3))

"The summons" is the court summons that follows the black eye. The Scythian traveller Anacharsis, as the Greeks remembered him, said the same thing more briefly:

> Οὗτος τὴν ἄμπελον εἶπε τρεῖς φέρειν βότρυς· τὸν πρῶτον ἡδονῆς· τὸν δεύτερον μέθης· τὸν τρίτον ἀηδίας.
> "He said the vine bears three bunches of grapes: the first of pleasure, the second of drunkenness, the third of disgust."
> ([Diogenes Laertius 1.8.103](read:tlg0004.tlg001:1.8.103))

## How a symposium worked

The guests, all men, reclined two to a couch on their left elbows in the ἀνδρών, the men's dining room, with couches around the walls. After dinner the tables were cleared, the floor swept, the guests garlanded and perfumed, a libation poured, and a hymn sung. One man was chosen as symposiarch, "master of the drinking", to decide the mixture and the number of bowls. Cups went round from left to right. The only women present were hired entertainers: pipe-girls, dancers, acrobats, and ἑταῖραι, courtesans. Athens even capped their fees. Among the jobs of the city's street wardens:

> τάς τε αὐλητρίδας καὶ τὰς ψαλτρίας καὶ τὰς κιθαριστρίας οὗτοι σκοποῦσιν, ὅπως μὴ πλείονος ἢ δυεῖν δραχμαῖν μισθωθήσονται
> "They see to it that pipe-girls, harp-girls and lyre-girls are not hired for more than two drachmas."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 50.2](read:tlg0086.tlg003:50.2))

If several men wanted the same girl, the wardens drew lots. The poet Xenophanes, around 500 BCE, describes the ideal opening of the evening:

> νῦν γὰρ δὴ ζάπεδον καθαρὸν καὶ χεῖρες ἁπάντων καὶ κύλικες· πλεκτοὺς δʼ ἀμφιτιθεῖ στεφάνους, ἄλλος δʼ εὐῶδες μύρον ἐν φιάλῃ παρατείνει· κρατὴρ δʼ ἕστηκεν μεστὸς ἐυφροσύνης
> "Now the floor is clean, and everyone's hands, and the cups. One boy sets woven garlands on our heads, another passes round sweet perfume in a bowl, and the mixing bowl stands full of good cheer."
> ([Athenaeus 11.7](read:tlg0008.tlg001:11.7))

## Games

Guests sang in turn, passing a myrtle branch; they told riddles and capped each other's verses; losers drank a penalty cup. The favourite game was κότταβος, from Sicily: you flicked the last drops of wine from your cup, with a twist of the wrist, at a target, often a small disc balanced on a stand, which had to fall with a clang onto a larger one below. You named your beloved as you threw, and a good shot was a good omen for love. Vase painters loved to show it.

## Plato's symposium

The most famous drinking party in literature, [Plato](author:tlg0059)'s *Symposium*, starts by breaking the rules. The guests are hung over from the night before, so they agree to drink only as much as they like, and to send the pipe-girl away:

> τὴν μὲν ἄρτι εἰσελθοῦσαν αὐλητρίδα χαίρειν ἐᾶν, αὐλοῦσαν ἑαυτῇ ἢ ἂν βούληται ταῖς γυναιξὶ ταῖς ἔνδον, ἡμᾶς δὲ διὰ λόγων ἀλλήλοις συνεῖναι τὸ τήμερον
> "Let the pipe-girl who has just come in go: she can play to herself, or to the women in the house if she likes. Let us spend today together in talk."
> ([Plato, *Symposium* 176e](read:tlg0059.tlg011:176e))

They make speeches about love instead, until a very drunk Alcibiades bursts in and the drinking begins in earnest. Alcibiades says of Socrates that he could drink anyone under the table and

> Σωκράτη μεθύοντα οὐδεὶς πώποτε ἑώρακεν ἀνθρώπων.
> "No one has ever seen Socrates drunk."
> ([Plato, *Symposium* 220a](read:tlg0059.tlg011:220a))

The dialogue proves it. At dawn, with the others asleep on the couches, Socrates gets up, goes to the Lyceum for a wash, and spends the day as usual.
