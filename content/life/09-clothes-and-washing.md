# Clothes and washing

Greek clothes were mostly rectangles of wool or linen, woven at home, wrapped and pinned rather than cut and sewn. People washed with water and olive oil, scraped themselves clean with a curved metal blade, and went to the barber as much for the gossip as for the haircut.

---

## Wrapped, not tailored

The basic garment was the χιτών, a tunic of two pieces of cloth joined at the sides and fastened at the shoulders, belted at the waist. Over it went the ἱμάτιον, a large woollen mantle draped round the body, which doubled as a blanket at night. Women wore their tunics to the ankle, working men to the knee, and a poor man might own a single cloak. Young men riding or travelling wore a short cape, the χλαμύς, and a broad-brimmed hat.

All of it was made at home. Spinning and weaving took up much of a woman's day, whatever her class, and a finished cloak represented weeks of work. Stealing clothes from the bath house was a recognised crime.

## Death by brooch-pin

Women's dress had once been fastened with long, sharp pins, and [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) tells how that changed at Athens. After a disastrous expedition against Aegina, around 500 BCE, only one Athenian came home alive:

> πυθομένας δὲ τὰς γυναῖκας τῶν ἐπʼ Αἴγιναν στρατευσαμένων ἀνδρῶν, δεινόν τι ποιησαμένας κεῖνον μοῦνον ἐξ ἁπάντων σωθῆναι, πέριξ τὸν ἄνθρωπον τοῦτον λαβούσας καὶ κεντεύσας τῇσι περόνῃσι τῶν ἱματίων εἰρωτᾶν ἑκάστην αὐτέων ὅκου εἴη ὁ ἑωυτῆς ἀνήρ.
> "When the wives of the men who had gone to Aegina heard, they were outraged that he alone of them all had survived. They surrounded him and stabbed him with the pins of their dresses, each one asking him where her own husband was."
> ([Herodotus 5.87.2](read:tlg0016.tlg001:5.87.2))

Unable to think of any other punishment, the Athenians made their women change to the Ionian linen tunic, sewn at the shoulders, "so that they would not use pins". Herodotus adds, pedantically, that the "Ionian" dress was really Carian.

## Barefoot and unwashed

Going barefoot was normal indoors and common outside; shoes and sandals were for the street, the road, and the well-to-do. Toughness about it was admired. At Potidaea, in a northern winter so cold that other soldiers wrapped their feet in felt and fleece, Socrates marched

> ἔχων ἱμάτιον μὲν τοιοῦτον οἷόνπερ καὶ πρότερον εἰώθει φορεῖν, ἀνυπόδητος δὲ διὰ τοῦ κρυστάλλου ῥᾷον ἐπορεύετο ἢ οἱ ἄλλοι ὑποδεδεμένοι
> "in the same cloak he always wore, and barefoot he walked over the ice more easily than the others in their shoes."
> ([Plato, *Symposium* 220b](read:tlg0059.tlg011:220b))

The soldiers, Alcibiades adds, thought he was showing off. Spartan boys, [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) says, went barefoot, cropped their hair, and lived "dirty, with no experience of baths or oil" except on a few days a year.

## Oil and the strigil

Soap as we know it was not used. To get clean after exercise, a man rubbed olive oil into his skin, let it mix with sweat and dust, and scraped the lot off with a στλεγγίς, a strigil: a curved bronze blade like a small sickle. Athletes' scrapings were collected, and the gunge, called γλοιός, was said to be good for the joints. A bottle of oil and a strigil hanging from a cord were the gym kit of every Greek man, and they appear on countless vases and gravestones.

Public baths spread in the fifth century: rows of hip-baths, or a round room with basins, heated water poured by an attendant. Old-fashioned people disapproved. In [Aristophanes](author:tlg0019)' *Clouds*, the voice of Right argues that hot baths ruin the young:

> ὅστις σε θερμῷ φησι λοῦσθαι πρῶτον οὐκ ἐάσειν. καίτοι τίνα γνώμην ἔχων ψέγεις τὰ θερμὰ λουτρά; — ὁτιὴ κάκιστόν ἐστι καὶ δειλὸν ποιεῖ τὸν ἄνδρα.
> "He says he won't let you have a hot bath, for a start. And what's your reason for condemning hot baths?" "Because it is the worst thing there is, and makes a man a coward."
> ([Aristophanes, *Clouds* 1044–1046](read:tlg0019.tlg003:1044))

Wrong replies that Heracles, the toughest man who ever lived, had hot springs named after him.

## Hair, beards and make-up

Grown men wore beards until Alexander the Great, who shaved, made a smooth face fashionable. Spartans wore their hair long, and before the last stand at Thermopylae in 480 BCE a Persian scout rode up to watch them:

> τοὺς μὲν δὴ ὥρα γυμναζομένους τῶν ἀνδρῶν, τοὺς δὲ τὰς κόμας κτενιζομένους.
> "He saw some of the men exercising, and others combing their hair."
> ([Herodotus 7.208.3](read:tlg0016.tlg001:7.208.3))

King Xerxes found it ridiculous. A Spartan exile explained that this was what Spartans did before they risked their lives.

Women whitened their faces with white lead, reddened their cheeks with alkanet root, and wore high shoes. [Xenophon](author:tlg0032)'s gentleman farmer found his wife made up in all three ways:

> ἐντετριμμένην πολλῷ μὲν ψιμυθίῳ, ὅπως λευκοτέρα ἔτι δοκοίη εἶναι ἢ ἦν, πολλῇ δʼ ἐγχούσῃ, ὅπως ἐρυθροτέρα φαίνοιτο τῆς ἀληθείας, ὑποδήματα δʼ ἔχουσαν ὑψηλά, ὅπως μείζων δοκοίη εἶναι ἢ ἐπεφύκει
> "plastered with white lead to look paler than she was, with plenty of rouge to look rosier than the truth, and wearing high shoes to look taller than nature made her."
> ([Xenophon, *Economics* 10.2](read:tlg0032.tlg003:10.2))

He talked her out of it, he says. White lead is poisonous, and Greek doctors knew it could kill if swallowed, yet it stayed in face powder for more than two thousand years.

## At the barber's

The barber's shop was where men went to hear the news, and barbers were famous talkers. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) has two stories in his essay on chatterboxes. When a talkative barber asked King Archelaus of Macedon how he would like his hair cut, the king said:

> σιωπῶν
> "In silence."
> ([Plutarch, *On Talkativeness* 13](read:tlg0007.tlg101:13))

The other is darker. A barber in the Piraeus heard from a runaway slave that the whole Athenian army in Sicily had been destroyed, and ran up to the city to be first with the news. No one had heard it; he could not name his source; the crowd had him tied to the torture wheel as a rumour-monger. Then the real messengers arrived, and everyone went home to mourn, leaving him on the wheel. Released at last, towards evening, he asked the public slave whether they had heard how the general Nicias had died.
