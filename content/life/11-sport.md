# Sport

Greek athletes competed naked and oiled, for a wreath of leaves, in games held in honour of the gods. The prizes at home were anything but symbolic, cheats paid for statues of Zeus, and one wrestler won an Olympic title after he was dead.

---

## Naked and oiled

The word γυμνάσιον comes from γυμνός, "naked", and Greek men exercised and competed without clothes. [Thucydides](author:tlg0003) says the custom was fairly new in his day, and that the Spartans started it:

> ἐγυμνώθησάν τε πρῶτοι καὶ ἐς τὸ φανερὸν ἀποδύντες λίπα μετὰ τοῦ γυμνάζεσθαι ἠλείψαντο· τὸ δὲ πάλαι καὶ ἐν τῷ Ὀλυμπικῷ ἀγῶνι διαζώματα ἔχοντες περὶ τὰ αἰδοῖα οἱ ἀθληταὶ ἠγωνίζοντο, καὶ οὐ πολλὰ ἔτη ἐπειδὴ πέπαυται.
> "They were the first to strip, undress in public and rub themselves with oil for exercise. In the old days even at the Olympic games athletes competed wearing loincloths, and it is not many years since that stopped."
> ([Thucydides 1.6.5](read:tlg0003.tlg001:1.6.5))

Other Greeks told a different story: that a runner called Orsippus lost his loincloth during a race in 720 BCE and won anyway. Either way, nudity marked the Greeks off from "barbarians", who, Thucydides adds, still wore loincloths for boxing and wrestling. After exercise came the oil flask and the strigil (see [Clothes and washing](09-clothes-and-washing.md)).

## The games

The Olympic games, held every four years at the sanctuary of Zeus at Olympia, were traditionally founded in 776 BCE; that date is late and learned, but the games are certainly old. With the Pythian games at Delphi, the Isthmian at Corinth and the Nemean, they made a circuit, and a man who won at all four was a περιοδονίκης, "circuit winner". A sacred truce let athletes and spectators travel safely.

The events: foot races of one length of the stadium (about 192 metres at Olympia), two lengths, and a long race; a race in armour; the pentathlon (running, long jump with hand weights, discus, javelin, wrestling); boxing with leather thongs round the knuckles; wrestling; the πανκράτιον, a free fight in which only biting and gouging were banned; and the chariot and horse races, which the owner won, not the driver.

## A crown of olive, and free dinners for life

The prize at Olympia was a wreath of wild olive. Home was another matter. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) says Solon fixed a reward of 500 drachmas for an Athenian Olympic victor, a small fortune, and Athens gave its victors free meals for life in the Prytaneion, the city's town hall. Sentenced to death and invited to propose an alternative penalty, Socrates suggested he deserved the same:

> ὁ μὲν γὰρ ὑμᾶς ποιεῖ εὐδαίμονας δοκεῖν εἶναι, ἐγὼ δὲ εἶναι, καὶ ὁ μὲν τροφῆς οὐδὲν δεῖται, ἐγὼ δὲ δέομαι.
> "He makes you think you are happy; I make you happy. And he does not need feeding; I do."
> ([Plato, *Apology* 36d–e](read:tlg0059.tlg002:36d))

The jury was not amused. The poet [Pindar](author:tlg0033) was paid to write odes for the winners, which were sung by choruses at victory celebrations. The first of his Olympian odes opens:

> ἄριστον μὲν ὕδωρ, ὁ δὲ χρυσὸς αἰθόμενον πῦρ ἅτε διαπρέπει νυκτὶ μεγάνορος ἔξοχα πλούτου
> "Best of all is water, and gold, like a fire blazing in the night, outshines all proud wealth."
> ([Pindar, *Olympian* 1.1–2](read:tlg0033.tlg001:1.1))

## Women keep out

Married women were forbidden to watch the Olympic games, on pain of death. [Pausanias](author:tlg0525), who visited in the second century CE, was shown the cliff:

> ὀνομάζεται δὲ Τυπαῖον τὸ ὄρος. κατὰ τούτου τὰς γυναῖκας Ἠλείοις ἐστὶν ὠθεῖν νόμος, ἢν φωραθῶσιν ἐς τὸν ἀγῶνα ἐλθοῦσαι τὸν Ὀλυμπικὸν
> "The mountain is called Typaeum. The Eleans have a law that women are to be thrown off it if they are caught coming to the Olympic games."
> ([Pausanias 5.6.7](read:tlg0525.tlg001:5.6.7))

No one was ever caught, he says, except one widow, Callipateira, who disguised herself as a trainer to watch her son box. When he won she jumped the barrier and her clothes slipped. She was let off, because her father, brothers and son were all Olympic champions, but from then on trainers had to attend naked too.

Unmarried girls could watch, and there was a separate footrace for girls at Olympia in honour of Hera. And a woman could win: owners, not drivers, took the prize for chariot racing, and in 396 BCE the Spartan princess Cynisca became the first woman to win at Olympia, with her team of horses.

## Cheats and champions

Bribery happened. Athletes caught fixing a contest were fined, and the fines paid for bronze statues of Zeus, the Ζᾶνες, which lined the path to the stadium, each inscribed with the offence:

> ταῦτα ἐποιήθη μὲν ἀπὸ χρημάτων ἐπιβληθείσης ἀθληταῖς ζημίας ὑβρίσασιν ἐς τὸν ἀγῶνα, καλοῦνται δὲ ὑπὸ τῶν ἐπιχωρίων Ζᾶνες.
> "These were made from the fines imposed on athletes who cheated in the games, and the locals call them Zanes."
> ([Pausanias 5.21.2](read:tlg0525.tlg001:5.21.2))

The first six were paid for by a boxer who bribed three opponents in 388 BCE. Their bases can still be seen.

Champions became legends. [Athenaeus](author:tlg0008) repeats what was told of Milo of Croton, six times Olympic wrestling champion in the sixth century BCE:

> ἤσθιε μνᾶς κρεῶν εἴκοσι καὶ τοσαύτας ἄρτων οἴνου τε τρεῖς χοᾶς ἔπινεν. ἐν δὲ Ὀλυμπίᾳ ταῦρον ἀναθέμενος τοῖς ὤμοις τετραέτη καὶ τοῦτον περιενέγκας τὸ στάδιον μετὰ ταῦτα δαιτρεύσας μόνος αὐτὸν κατέφαγεν ἐν μιᾷ ἡμέρᾳ.
> "He ate twenty minas of meat (some nine kilos) and as much bread, and drank three jugs of wine. At Olympia he put a four-year-old bull on his shoulders, carried it round the stadium, then carved it up and ate it on his own in a single day."
> ([Athenaeus 10.4](read:tlg0008.tlg001:10.4))

The story that he trained by carrying a calf every day until it was a bull is later still. Treat all of it as legend.

## Victory in death

The strangest Olympic result is told by Pausanias. The pancratiast Arrhichion, going for his third title in 564 BCE, was caught in a stranglehold with his opponent's legs locked round his waist:

> ὁ δὲ Ἀρραχίων ἐκκλᾷ τῶν ἐν τῷ ποδὶ τοῦ ἀνταγωνιζομένου δάκτυλον, καὶ Ἀρραχίων τε τὴν ψυχὴν ἀφίησιν ἀγχόμενος καὶ ὁ ἄγχων τὸν Ἀρραχίωνα ὑπὸ τοῦ δακτύλου τῆς ὀδύνης κατὰ τὸν καιρὸν ἀπαγορεύει τὸν αὐτόν.
> "Arrhichion dislocated one of his opponent's toes. Arrhichion died of strangling, and at the same moment the man strangling him gave up because of the pain in his toe."
> ([Pausanias 8.40.2](read:tlg0525.tlg001:8.40.2))

The judges crowned the corpse.
