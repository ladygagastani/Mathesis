# Exposure, exile and punishment

Some things in Greek life are hard to read about. Unwanted babies could be left out to die. Citizens could be sent into exile by a vote, sometimes for being too admired. And the law, before Solon, could sell a debtor into slavery; after Draco, it could put a man to death for stealing cabbages, or so the Greeks later believed.

---

## Babies left outside

A Greek father decided whether a newborn would be raised. If not, the child was "put out", ἐκτίθεσθαι: left in a pot or a basket at the edge of town, on a hillside or near a shrine. Some were taken in by strangers and raised, often as slaves; most, presumably, died. Myth is full of exposed babies who survived to become heroes or kings: Oedipus, left on Mount Cithaeron with his ankles pinned; Ion, left in a cave by his mother; Cyrus the Great of Persia, whose story [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) tells at length. The stories take the practice for granted.

Philosophers designing ideal cities discussed it coolly. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086) proposes:

> περὶ δὲ ἀποθέσεως καὶ τροφῆς τῶν γιγνομένων ἔστω νόμος μηδὲν πεπηρωμένον τρέφειν
> "As for exposing or rearing children, let there be a law that no deformed child shall be reared."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 7.1335b](read:tlg0086.tlg035:7.1335b))

Sparta, [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) says, left the decision to the elders of the tribe, who sent a weak baby to the Apothetae, "the Place of Putting Away", a ravine on Mount Taygetus (see [Childhood and school](04-childhood-and-school.md)). No infant bones have been found there; a pit investigated in the twentieth century held adults.

How common exposure was, historians disagree. Some think it was frequent, especially for girls; others that it was rare and mostly for the poorest, the illegitimate and the sickly. A letter on papyrus from Roman Egypt, dated 1 BCE, in which a man away working tells his pregnant wife "if it is a boy, keep it; if it is a girl, throw it out", is often quoted. It is one letter.

## Sold for debt

In early Athens a poor farmer who could not pay his debts could be seized, with his wife and children, and sold as a slave. Solon, chosen to mediate between rich and poor in 594 BCE, stopped it. [Aristotle](author:tlg0086)'s history of the Athenian constitution says:

> κύριος δὲ γενόμενος τῶν πραγμάτων Σόλων τόν τε δῆμον ἠλευθέρωσε καὶ ἐν τῷ παρόντι καὶ εἰς τὸ μέλλον, κωλύσας δανείζειν ἐπὶ τοῖς σώμασιν
> "Once in charge, Solon set the people free, both for the moment and for the future, by forbidding loans made on the security of the person."
> ([Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 6.1](read:tlg0086.tlg003:6.1))

He also cancelled debts, the "shaking-off of burdens", σεισάχθεια, and brought back Athenians who had been sold abroad. Debt slavery for citizens never returned at Athens, though it continued elsewhere.

## Laws written in blood

Solon's predecessor Draco, around 620 BCE, gave Athens its first written laws, and his name became a byword. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) repeats what later Athenians believed:

> μία γὰρ ὀλίγου δεῖν ἅπασιν ὥριστο τοῖς ἁμαρτάνουσι ζημία θάνατος, ὥστε καὶ τοὺς ἀργίας ἁλόντας ἀποθνήσκειν, καὶ τοὺς λάχανα κλέψαντας ἢ ὀπώραν ὁμοίως κολάζεσθαι τοῖς ἱεροσύλοις καὶ ἀνδροφόνοις. διὸ Δημάδης ὕστερον εὐδοκίμησεν εἰπὼν ὅτι διʼ αἵματος, οὐ διὰ μέλανος, τοὺς νόμους ὁ Δράκων ἔγραψεν.
> "One penalty was laid down for almost every offence: death. Even those convicted of idleness were put to death, and those who stole vegetables or fruit were punished like temple-robbers and murderers. That is why Demades later won fame for saying that Draco wrote his laws not in ink but in blood."
> ([Plutarch, *Solon* 17.1–2](read:tlg0007.tlg007:17.1))

Asked why, Draco supposedly said that small crimes deserved death, and he had nothing bigger for the big ones. Only his homicide law survives, on an inscription of 409 BCE, and it is not especially bloodthirsty: it is the first Greek law to distinguish between deliberate and accidental killing.

## Voted out

Exile was one of the heaviest punishments a Greek could suffer: it meant losing home, property, friends, gods and the protection of the law. In Homer a man who killed someone, even by accident, fled abroad; Patroclus came to Achilles' house that way, after killing another boy over a game (see [Games, pets and jokes](15-games-pets-and-jokes.md)). The historian [Thucydides](author:tlg0003), a general who failed to save the city of Amphipolis, spent twenty years in exile, and made the best of it:

> καὶ ξυνέβη μοι φεύγειν τὴν ἐμαυτοῦ ἔτη εἴκοσι μετὰ τὴν ἐς Ἀμφίπολιν στρατηγίαν, καὶ γενομένῳ παρʼ ἀμφοτέροις τοῖς πράγμασι, καὶ οὐχ ἧσσον τοῖς Πελοποννησίων διὰ τὴν φυγήν, καθʼ ἡσυχίαν τι αὐτῶν μᾶλλον αἰσθέσθαι.
> "It happened that I was exiled from my own country for twenty years after my command at Amphipolis, and being present on both sides, and not least on the Peloponnesian side because of my exile, I had the leisure to understand events better."
> ([Thucydides 5.26.5](read:tlg0003.tlg001:5.26.5))

Athens also had a peculiar kind of exile with no crime at all: ostracism. Once a year the Assembly could vote to hold one. Each citizen scratched a name on a potsherd, an ὄστρακον; if at least six thousand votes were cast, the man with the most had to leave Attica for ten years, though he kept his property. Thousands of these sherds have been found. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) tells the most famous story about it, from the ostracism of Aristides, nicknamed "the Just", in 482 BCE:

> λέγεταί τινα τῶν ἀγραμμάτων καὶ παντελῶς ἀγροίκων ἀναδόντα τῷ Ἀριστείδῃ τὸ ὄστρακον ὡς ἑνὶ τῶν τυχόντων παρακαλεῖν, ὅπως Ἀριστείδην ἐγγράψειε.
> "It is said that an illiterate, thoroughly rustic man handed his sherd to Aristides, taking him for just anyone, and asked him to write 'Aristides' on it."
> ([Plutarch, *Aristides* 7.5](read:tlg0007.tlg024:7.5))

Aristides asked whether Aristides had done him any harm. "None," the man said, "I don't even know him. I'm just sick of hearing him called 'the Just' everywhere." Aristides wrote his own name and handed it back. Whether it happened we cannot tell; the ostraka themselves show that some voters needed help, since a batch of 190 sherds against Themistocles, found in a well on the Acropolis slope, were written by only a few hands, ready to hand out.

## Execution

Athens had no prison sentences; the jail held people awaiting trial, fine payment or execution. Punishments were fines, loss of citizen rights (ἀτιμία), exile or death. Death might mean being thrown into a pit, the βάραθρον; being clamped to a wooden plank and left to die, ἀποτυμπανισμός; or, for citizens, drinking hemlock. [Plato](author:tlg0059) describes how Socrates walked about after drinking it until his legs grew heavy, then lay down, and the attendant pressed his feet and shins:

> καὶ ἐπανιὼν οὕτως ἡμῖν ἐπεδείκνυτο ὅτι ψύχοιτό τε καὶ πήγνυτο. καὶ αὐτὸς ἥπτετο καὶ εἶπεν ὅτι, ἐπειδὰν πρὸς τῇ καρδίᾳ γένηται αὐτῷ, τότε οἰχήσεται.
> "Moving upwards in this way, he showed us that he was growing cold and stiff. He touched him himself, and said that when it reached his heart, he would be gone."
> ([Plato, *Phaedo* 118a](read:tlg0059.tlg004:118))

Whether hemlock really kills so gently has been argued over: some think Plato softened his teacher's death, others that his account matches the poison closely.
