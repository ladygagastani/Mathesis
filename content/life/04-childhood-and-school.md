# Childhood and school

A Greek baby was not fully part of the family until its father accepted it, a few days after birth. If it was, it grew up with rattles and knucklebones, a slave to walk it to school, and lessons that were mostly poetry, music and exercise. Schools were private, and could be dangerous places.

---

## Running round the hearth

Around the fifth day after a birth came the ἀμφιδρόμια, "running around": the baby was carried round the hearth, a sign that it now belonged to the house. The naming feast followed on the tenth day. Socrates, who liked to call himself a midwife of ideas, uses the ritual as a joke about a newborn theory:

> μετὰ δὲ τὸν τόκον τὰ ἀμφιδρόμια αὐτοῦ ὡς ἀληθῶς ἐν κύκλῳ περιθρεκτέον τῷ λόγῳ, σκοπουμένους μὴ λάθῃ ἡμᾶς οὐκ ἄξιον ὂν τροφῆς τὸ γιγνόμενον
> "Now that it is born we must truly run it round the hearth of our argument, and check that we have not failed to notice it is not worth rearing."
> ([Plato, *Theaetetus* 160e–161a](read:tlg0059.tlg006:161))

"Not worth rearing" is not only a figure of speech. A father could refuse a child, and the grim side of that is told in [Exposure, exile and punishment](19-exposure-exile-punishment.md). Many children who were kept did not live long: modern estimates, based on cemeteries and on comparison with other societies before modern medicine, suggest that a quarter to a third died in their first year.

## Rattles and toys

[Aristotle](author:tlg0086) thought small children needed something to do with their hands, and gave the credit for the baby rattle to a philosopher:

> καὶ τὴν Ἀρχύτου πλαταγὴν οἴεσθαι γενέσθαι καλῶς, ἣν διδόασι τοῖς παιδίοις, ὅπως χρώμενοι ταύτῃ μηδὲν καταγνύωσι τῶν κατὰ τὴν οἰκίαν· οὐ γὰρ δύναται τὸ νέον ἡσυχάζειν.
> "Archytas' rattle was a fine invention, given to children so that, busy with it, they do not break anything in the house; for the young cannot keep still."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 8.1340b](read:tlg0086.tlg035:8.1340b.ross_section_6))

Archytas was a mathematician and general in Tarentum, a friend of Plato. Whether he really invented the rattle, or only a better one, we cannot tell: clay rattles are much older. Children also had dolls with jointed limbs, spinning tops, hoops, carts, balls, see-saws, and above all knucklebones (see [Games, pets and jokes](15-games-pets-and-jokes.md)). Tiny jugs painted with toddlers crawling and playing were given to Athenian three-year-olds at a spring festival of wine, the Anthesteria; many were buried with children who died.

## The walk to school

There was no public schooling at Athens. Families who could afford it paid teachers, and a slave called the παιδαγωγός, "child-leader", walked the boy there, carried his things and kept an eye on his manners. (Our *pedagogue* is a promotion.) In [Plato](author:tlg0059)'s *Protagoras* the sophist describes an Athenian upbringing:

> καὶ τροφὸς καὶ μήτηρ καὶ παιδαγωγὸς καὶ αὐτὸς ὁ πατὴρ περὶ τούτου διαμάχονται, ὅπως ὡς βέλτιστος ἔσται ὁ παῖς … εἰ δὲ μή, ὥσπερ ξύλον διαστρεφόμενον καὶ καμπτόμενον εὐθύνουσιν ἀπειλαῖς καὶ πληγαῖς.
> "Nurse and mother and tutor and father himself vie to make the boy as good as can be … and if he will not obey, they straighten him like a bent and twisted piece of wood, with threats and beatings."
> ([Plato, *Protagoras* 325c–d](read:tlg0059.tlg022:325c))

Then came three teachers: the γραμματιστής for letters, who had the boys learn Homer by heart; the κιθαριστής for the lyre; and the παιδοτρίβης for wrestling and exercise. Girls were mostly taught at home, spinning and weaving, though vase paintings show some women reading.

## "Even if it snowed like flour"

[Aristophanes](author:tlg0019) has an old-fashioned speaker in *Clouds* remember the good old days:

> εἶτα βαδίζειν ἐν ταῖσιν ὁδοῖς εὐτάκτως ἐς κιθαριστοῦ τοὺς κωμήτας γυμνοὺς ἁθρόους, κεἰ κριμνώδη κατανείφοι.
> "The boys of each neighbourhood walked through the streets to the music teacher in good order, all together, lightly dressed, even if it snowed like barley meal."
> ([Aristophanes, *Clouds* 964–965](read:tlg0019.tlg003:964))

The same speaker complains that boys nowadays snatch the best bits at dinner and sit with their legs crossed. Adults have complained about the young in every age for which we have records.

## Schools that fell in

Classrooms appear in Greek history mostly when something went wrong. [Herodotus](author:tlg0016) lists the omens before the island of Chios fell to the Persians in 494 BCE:

> παισὶ γράμματα διδασκομένοισι ἐνέπεσε ἡ στέγη, ὥστε ἀπʼ ἑκατὸν καὶ εἴκοσι παίδων εἷς μοῦνος ἀπέφυγε.
> "The roof fell in on the boys as they were being taught their letters, so that out of a hundred and twenty children only one escaped."
> ([Herodotus 6.27.2](read:tlg0016.tlg001:6.27.2))

It is also the earliest mention of a school anywhere in Greek. [Pausanias](author:tlg0525) tells a worse story, of a boxer, Cleomedes of Astypalaea, who was stripped of an Olympic victory, went mad with grief, and at home

> διδασκαλείῳ δʼ ἐπιστὰς ἐνταῦθα ὅσον ἑξήκοντα ἀριθμὸν παίδων ἀνατρέπει τὸν κίονα ὃς τὸν ὄροφον ἀνεῖχεν.
> "came to a school where there were about sixty children, and pulled down the pillar that held up the roof."
> ([Pausanias 6.9.6](read:tlg0525.tlg001:6.9.6))

## Growing up Spartan

At Sparta the state took over. [Plutarch](author:tlg0007) says that a newborn was examined by the elders of the tribe, and a weak one was taken to a ravine on Mount Taygetus. Boys left home at seven to live in packs and went barefoot; from twelve they were given one cloak a year and no tunic. They were fed so little that they had to steal, and were beaten if caught. The famous example:

> λέγεταί τίς ἤδη σκύμνον ἀλώπεκος κεκλοφὼς καὶ τῷ τριβωνίῳ περιστέλλων, σπαρασσόμενος ὑπὸ τοῦ θηρίου τὴν γαστέρα τοῖς ὄνυξι καὶ τοῖς ὀδοῦσιν, ὑπὲρ τοῦ λαθεῖν ἐγκαρτερῶν ἀποθανεῖν.
> "One boy, they say, had stolen a fox cub and hid it under his cloak; it tore at his belly with its claws and teeth, and he let himself die rather than be found out."
> ([Plutarch, *Lycurgus* 18.1](read:tlg0007.tlg004:18.1))

"They say": Plutarch is passing on a story, written many centuries after the laws it illustrates. But he adds, of the boys he had seen whipped at the altar of Artemis Orthia in his own day, "we have seen many of them die under the blows."
