# A lookup, step by step

This page looks up two real words from two famous openings. The first is a warm-up. The
second is a verb whose form gives almost no clue to its dictionary word, which is exactly
why it is worth doing slowly.

## Warm-up: ἄνδρα in the first line of the *Odyssey*

> ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, μοῦσα, πολύτροπον
> "Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns."\* ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.1](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.1))

**What you see:** ἄνδρα, ending in -α. You might guess a nominative like "ἄνδρος" and search
for it in vain. Click the word in the reader and Logeion takes you to the dictionary form:

> **ἀνήρ, ἀνδρός, ὁ**: man

The genitive ἀνδρός shows the stem ἀνδρ-, and ἄνδρα is ἀνδρ- plus the accusative ending
-α. It is the object of "tell me (about)". The article ὁ tells you it is masculine.

**Reading the entry.** The Middle Liddell begins: "a man, Lat. *vir* (not *homo*)". The Latin
is the key. *Vir* is a man as opposed to a woman: a male, a husband, a hero. *Homo* is a
human being. Greek makes the same split: ἀνήρ *anēr* is the man, ἄνθρωπος *anthrōpos* the
human being. The entry then lists the senses, each labelled by what it is contrasted with:

- **I.** "a man, opp. to a woman"
- **II.** "opp. to a god"
- **III.** "opp. to a youth"
- **IV.** "a man emphatically, a man indeed"
- **V.** "opp. to his wife, a husband"

("opp." means "opposed to".) The Middle Liddell illustrates sense IV with Herodotus. At
Thermopylae, the failed Persian assault made it plain to everyone, the king included, "ὅτι
πολλοὶ μὲν ἄνθρωποι εἶεν, ὀλίγοι δὲ ἄνδρες", that "they were many human beings, but few
men" ([Herodotus 7.210](read:tlg0016.tlg001:7.210)). Both words are at work in one sentence.

## The main lookup: πεπόνθατε in Plato's *Apology*

Socrates' speech in his own defence opens like this:

> ὅτι μὲν ὑμεῖς, ὦ ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι, **πεπόνθατε** ὑπὸ τῶν ἐμῶν κατηγόρων, οὐκ οἶδα
> "How you, men of Athens, have been affected by my accusers, I do not know."
> ([Plato, *Apology* 17a](read:tlg0059.tlg002:17a))

(ἄνδρες is our ἀνήρ again, now plural and addressed directly: "men!")

### Step 1: look at the form before you look it up

Break the word into pieces: **πε · πόνθ · ατε**.

- **πε-** at the front: the first consonant, π, repeated with ε. This is **reduplication**
  (page 4), the usual sign of a **perfect** tense ("have done").
- **-ατε** at the end is a perfect ending for "you" in the plural. Compare λελύκατε "you
  have loosened".
- **πονθ-** is left in the middle. No dictionary word begins πονθ-, or πεπονθ-.

So we know it means "you (plural) have …ed", but not which verb.

### Step 2: let a tool find the headword

Click the word and choose **Logeion**. It reports: *"Could not find πεπόνθατε in Logeion
dictionaries. Parsed as a form of: πάσχω."* The **Perseus Word Study Tool** gives the full
analysis: perfect, indicative, active, second person plural, of **πάσχω**.

The dictionary word is **πάσχω** *paskhō*. Its principal parts are πάσχω, πείσομαι,
ἔπαθον, πέπονθα. None of the letters π-ο-ν-θ appears in πάσχω, which is why looking it up
by guesswork would have failed.

### Step 3: read the top of the LSJ entry

The entry in LSJ opens with a block of forms:

> πάσχω, Il. 20.297, etc.: impf. ἔπασχον 17.375, etc.: fut. πείσομαι Od. 2.134, etc.; …
> aor. ἔπᾰθον Il. 9.492, etc.: pf. πέπονθα Od. 13.6, etc.: plpf. ἐπεπόνθειν ib. 92, etc.;
> Att. ἐπεπόνθη Pl. Smp. 198c (all the above tenses in Hom., pres. and aor. only in Hes.).

Reading it piece by piece:

- **πάσχω, Il. 20.297**: the headword, with a place where it occurs: *Iliad* book 20, line
  297.
- **impf. ἔπασχον 17.375**: the imperfect ("I was suffering"). The bare "17.375" means the
  same work as before: *Iliad* 17.375.
- **fut. πείσομαι Od. 2.134**: the future, cited from the *Odyssey*.
- **aor. ἔπᾰθον**: the aorist. The little cup over α (a breve) tells you it is short.
- **pf. πέπονθα Od. 13.6**: **the perfect: our form comes from here.** πέπονθ-α "I have
  suffered" becomes πεπόνθ-ατε "you have suffered".
- **plpf. … ib. 92**: the pluperfect ("I had suffered"). *ib.* (Latin *ibidem*, "in the same
  place") means the same work and book: *Odyssey* 13.92.
- **Att. … Pl. Smp. 198c**: an Attic form, cited from Plato's *Symposium*.
- **(all the above tenses in Hom., pres. and aor. only in Hes.)**: a note on where the
  forms occur. All of them are in Homer; only the present and aorist in Hesiod.

Skip the "rarer forms" that follow. They matter only to specialists.

### Step 4: find the right meaning

LSJ numbers the meanings with Roman numerals (I, II, III), then Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3)
for sub-senses.

- **I.** "have something done to one, suffer, opp. do". The basic idea: πάσχω is the
  opposite of doing. LSJ adds that πάσχω τι ὑπό τινος means "to be treated so and so by
  another". Our sentence has exactly that construction: ὑπὸ τῶν ἐμῶν κατηγόρων, "by my
  accusers".
- **II. 1.** "to have something happen to one, to be or come to be in a state or case".
- **II. 2.** "of the influence of passion or feeling, to be affected in a certain way". And
  here LSJ quotes *our sentence*: "ὅ τι μὲν ὑμεῖς πεπόνθατε ὑπὸ τῶν ἐμῶν κατηγόρων οὐκ
  οἶδα Pl. Ap. 17a".

This is the meaning we want. Socrates is not saying the jurors have "suffered" in the
English sense. He means that the prosecution speeches have *worked on* them. English
"suffer" once had this broader sense of "undergo", but πάσχω never lost it.

Notice one small difference: LSJ prints ὅ τι ("in what way"), while the edition in the app
prints ὅτι. Editions of the same text differ in details like this, and dictionaries quote
the editions that were standard when they were made.

### Step 5: put it together

> "How you, men of Athens, have been affected by my accusers, I do not know."

## A family of words

Once you know πάσχω and its aorist stem παθ-, a family of words opens up:
- πάθος *pathos*: "what happens to one; suffering; emotion". It gives English *pathos*,
  *pathetic*, *sympathy* (συμπάθεια, "feeling with") and *pathology*.
- Aeschylus sums up the law of Zeus as πάθει μάθος, "learning through suffering"
  ([*Agamemnon* 177](read:tlg0085.tlg005:177)).
- Herodotus has the defeated king Croesus say τὰ δέ μοι παθήματα ἐόντα ἀχάριτα μαθήματα
  γέγονε, "my sufferings, bitter as they were, have become my lessons" ([Herodotus 1.207](read:tlg0016.tlg001:1.207)).

The same μαθ- root, "learning", gives this app its name: **Μάθησις** *Mathēsis*, "learning".

## Practice: find the dictionary form

Each of these words comes from a passage in this guide. Give the dictionary form and say
what you think the form is. The first three are from *Iliad* 1.1–3, the next three from
*Odyssey* 1.3, the next three from Plato, *Apology* 17a, and the last from Aristotle,
*Politics* 1253a.

ἄειδε · ἔθηκε · ψυχάς · ἴδεν · ἔγνω · ἀνθρώπων · ἄνδρες · κατηγόρων · οἶδα · ἔχει

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. ἄειδε → **ἀείδω** "sing" (Attic ᾄδω): the command "sing!"
2. ἔθηκε → **τίθημι** "put, make, cause": aorist, "(it) caused". The subject is the wrath of line 1, and the augment ἐ- is a clue.
3. ψυχάς → **ψυχή, -ῆς, ἡ** "soul, life": accusative plural.
4. ἴδεν → **ὁράω** "see": aorist, "he saw". The Attic aorist is εἶδον; Homer often leaves off the augment. This is one of the principal-part traps from page 4.
5. ἔγνω → **γιγνώσκω** "come to know": aorist, "he learned".
6. ἀνθρώπων → **ἄνθρωπος, -ου, ὁ** "human being": genitive plural, "of men".
7. ἄνδρες → **ἀνήρ, ἀνδρός, ὁ** "man": vocative plural, "men!"
8. κατηγόρων → **κατήγορος, -ου, ὁ** "accuser": genitive plural. The related word κατηγορία gave English *category*.
9. οἶδα → **οἶδα** "I know". It is listed under itself: in form it is a perfect, but it means "I know" in the present.
10. ἔχει → **ἔχω** "have, hold": present, "(he) has".
</details>
