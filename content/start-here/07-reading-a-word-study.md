# How to read a word study

The next two pages follow twelve common Greek words through three thousand years, from
clay tablets to today's Athens. They were chosen because you will meet them in your first
week of reading, and because together they show every main way a language changes. This
page explains the terms used and the way each study is laid out.

## The layout of a study

Each study has the same parts:

1. **The word:** in Greek, transliterated, with its dictionary form and core meaning.
2. **Why this word:** one or two sentences on what it teaches.
3. **Where it comes from:** its etymology, marked for certainty.
4. **Through the eras:** what happened to it in each period. A period is skipped when there
   is nothing worth saying about it.
5. **In the texts:** short quotations with references. Click through to read them in the
   reader.
6. **In English:** words we took from it.

## Some terms

- **Etymology:** the history of a word's form and meaning. (From Greek ἔτυμον *etymon*, "the
  true sense of a word".)
- **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** the ancestor language of most languages of Europe, Iran
  and northern India, including Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, the Germanic languages (English
  among them), the Slavic and the Celtic languages. It was spoken perhaps five or six
  thousand years ago and never written down. Scholars reconstruct its words by comparing
  their descendants. An **asterisk** marks a reconstructed form that is not found in any
  text: \*ped- "foot".
- **Cognates** are words in different languages that descend from the same ancestor word.
  They are cousins, not parent and child. English *water* is not borrowed from Greek ὕδωρ;
  both descend from a PIE word for water. English *hydrant* **is** made from Greek: that is
  a **borrowing**, or **derivative**.
- **Certainty.** Etymologies are marked **✓** when scholars agree, and **?** when they are
  disputed or unknown. An honest "?" is better than a confident guess.

## Mycenaean Greek in Linear B

Several studies begin with a Linear B form. Linear B writes **syllables**, not single
sounds: each sign stands for a consonant plus a vowel (*ka*, *ko*, *te*…), or for a vowel
alone. It was designed for another language (the unknown language of Linear A), so it fits
Greek badly:

- **Consonants at the end of a syllable are left out.** *ka-ko* is *khalkos* "bronze",
  later χαλκός.
- **Clusters of consonants get extra vowels.** *ti-ri-po* is *tripos* "tripod".
- **Some sounds are not distinguished.** *r* and *l* share signs. Aspirated and plain
  consonants are written alike (*te-o* is *theos*, "god").
- **It records sounds that later Greek lost.** It had signs for *w* (*wo-ko* is *woikos*
  "house", later οἶκος), and for the sounds *kʷ* and *gʷ* (a *k* or *g* said with rounded
  lips). *qa-si-re-u* is *gʷasileus*, later βασιλεύς *basileus* "king".

So a Linear B spelling is a sketch of the word, and scholars recover the full form by
comparing it with later Greek. Linear B survives only in administrative records: lists of
people, land, livestock and goods. So we know the Mycenaean words for bronze, tripods and
land-holding, but almost nothing of their words for thought or feeling.

## Kinds of change

The studies show five kinds of change.

**1. Sound change.** Sounds change regularly: the same change hits every word with the same
sound in the same position. This is why cognates look different but correspond
systematically:

| Greek | Latin | English | Change in English |
|---|---|---|---|
| πούς, ποδός | *pēs, pedis* | foot | *p* → *f*, *d* → *t* |
| πατήρ | *pater* | father | *p* → *f*, *t* → *th* |
| τρεῖς | *trēs* | three | *t* → *th* |
| δέκα | *decem* | ten | *d* → *t* |

The English column shows **Grimm's law**, a set of consonant changes that happened in the
prehistory of the Germanic languages. Greek had regular changes of its own. For example, an
*s* at the start of a word became *h*: ἑπτά *hepta* but Latin *septem* "seven", ἅλς *hals*
but Latin *sāl* "salt".

**2. Meaning change.**
- **Widening:** a word comes to cover more. λόγος grows from "account, reckoning" to "word",
  "speech", "reason".
- **Narrowing** or **specialisation:** a word comes to cover less, or something more
  particular. πόλις "city" comes to mean one city, Constantinople.
- **Shift:** the meaning moves sideways. καλός "beautiful" becomes Modern Greek for "good".

**3. Replacement.** An everyday word is pushed out by a newcomer. In Greek the newcomer is
often one of these:
- a **diminutive** (a "little" form, like English *doggie*) that loses its smallness;
- a **loanword** from another language;
- a **phrase** worn down to one word.

The old word often survives in learned vocabulary. Modern Greek still uses *hydro-* words,
though the everyday word for water has changed.

**4. Survival.** Some words have barely changed in 3,000 years. The pronunciation moved on
(page 3), but the word stayed.

**5. Rebuilt forms.** Speakers reshape odd-looking words on the model of regular ones. This
is called **analogy**. Children do it in English when they say *goed* for *went*.

## For review (not for publication)
- **Linear B.** The examples (*ka-ko*, *ti-ri-po*, *te-o*, *wo-ko*, *qa-si-re-u*) and the spelling rules were checked against the Wikipedia articles *Linear B* and *Mycenaean Greek* and against Wiktionary. For publication, a standard handbook would be better: Ventris & Chadwick's *Documents in Mycenaean Greek*, or the *Companion to Linear B*.
- **"Designed for another language".** That Linear B was adapted from Linear A, written for a non-Greek language, is standard. It is the usual explanation for its poor fit to Greek.
- **PIE date.** "Five or six thousand years ago" is a deliberately vague round figure. The date of Proto-Indo-European is debated.
- **The Grimm's law table** simplifies. For example, Latin *pater* and English *father* also differ in their vowels. The correspondences shown are the standard textbook ones.
