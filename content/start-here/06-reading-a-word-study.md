# How to read a word study

The map for steps 7 and 8: the stages of Greek, the terms, and the kinds of change to watch
for.

Steps 7 and 8 follow twelve common words through 3,400 years. They were chosen because you
will meet them in your first week of reading, and because together they show every main way
a language changes.

## What each study contains

1. **The word**, with its dictionary form and meaning.
2. **Why this word:** what it teaches.
3. **Where it comes from:** its origin, marked ✓ when scholars agree and **?** when they do
   not.
4. **Through the eras:** what happened to it in each stage of Greek below.
5. **In the texts:** short quotations. Click a reference to read it in the reader.
6. **In English:** the words we took from it.

## The stages of Greek

Greek has been written, with gaps, for about 3,400 years: the longest written record of any
language still spoken in Europe.

| Stage | When | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Mycenaean** | c. 1400–1200 BCE | The oldest written Greek, on clay tablets in **Linear B** (below): palace records, no literature. Michael Ventris showed in 1952 that the language was Greek. |
| **Homeric** | the poems took shape c. 8th–7th century BCE | The language of epic: mostly Ionic, mixed with other dialects and very old words kept alive by oral poetry. Nobody ever spoke it at home. |
| **Classical** | 5th–4th century BCE | Each city-state had its own **dialect**: Attic (Athens), Ionic, Aeolic (Sappho), Doric (Pindar). Attic is the Greek of this guide. |
| **Koine** | c. 323 BCE – 4th century CE | *Koinē* means "common": after Alexander's conquests a simplified Attic became the shared language from Egypt to the Indus. The Septuagint (the Greek Old Testament) and the New Testament. |
| **Byzantine** | c. 4th century – 1453 | The Eastern Roman Empire. Speech moved towards Modern Greek, while many writers imitated Classical Attic. |
| **Modern** | after 1453 | The descendant of the spoken medieval language; standard as *Demotic* (everyday) Greek since 1976. |

In the app's Wiki, Homeric Greek falls in the Archaic era; Koine covers the Hellenistic and
Roman Imperial eras; Byzantine covers the Late Antique and Byzantine eras.

## Some terms

- **Etymology:** the history of a word's form and meaning.
- **Proto-Indo-European (PIE):** the ancestor of most languages of Europe, Iran and northern
  India, English, Greek, Latin and Sanskrit among them. It was spoken perhaps five or six
  thousand years ago and never written down. An **asterisk** marks a form rebuilt by
  comparing its descendants, not found in any text: \*ped- "foot".
- **Cognates** are words in different languages descended from the same ancestor: cousins,
  not parent and child. English *water* is not borrowed from Greek ὕδωρ; both descend from a
  PIE word. English *hydrant*, on the other hand, **is** made from Greek: that is a
  **borrowing**.

## Linear B

Several studies begin with a Linear B spelling. Linear B writes **syllables**: each sign
stands for a consonant plus a vowel (*ka*, *ko*, *te*…) or a vowel alone. It was made for
another language, so it fits Greek badly:

- **A consonant at the end of a syllable is left out:** *ka-ko* is *khalkos* "bronze".
- **Clusters get extra vowels:** *ti-ri-po* is *tripos* "tripod".
- ***r* and *l* share signs,** and breathy and plain consonants are written alike: *te-o* is
  *theos* "god".
- **It shows sounds later Greek lost:** a *w* (*wo-ko* is *woikos* "house", later οἶκος),
  and *kʷ*, a *k* said with rounded lips (*qa-si-re-u* is *gʷasileus*, later βασιλεύς
  "king").

So a Linear B spelling is a sketch of the word. And because the tablets are lists of people,
land, livestock and goods, we know the Mycenaean words for bronze and tripods, but almost
none for thought or feeling.

## Five kinds of change

**1. Sound change.** Sounds change regularly: the same change hits every word with that
sound in that position. This is why cognates look different but match systematically:

| Greek | Latin | English |
|---|---|---|
| πούς, ποδός | *pēs, pedis* | foot |
| πατήρ | *pater* | father |
| τρεῖς | *trēs* | three |
| δέκα | *decem* | ten |

In English, *p* became *f*, *t* became *th*, *d* became *t*: this set of changes is called
**Grimm's law**. Greek had its own. For instance, an *s* at the start of a word became *h*:
ἑπτά *hepta* but Latin *septem* "seven".

**2. Meaning change.**
- **Widening:** a word covers more. λόγος grows from "account" to "word", "speech", "reason".
- **Narrowing:** a word covers less. πόλις "city" comes to mean one city, Constantinople.
- **Shift:** the meaning moves sideways. καλός "beautiful" becomes Modern Greek for "good".

**3. Replacement.** An everyday word is pushed out by a newcomer: a "little" form (like
English *doggie*) that loses its smallness, a word borrowed from another language, or a
phrase worn down to one word. The old word often lives on in learned vocabulary.

**4. Survival.** Some words have barely changed in 3,000 years, apart from their sound.

**5. Rebuilt forms.** Speakers reshape odd words on the model of regular ones, as children
say *goed* for *went*. This is called **analogy**.
