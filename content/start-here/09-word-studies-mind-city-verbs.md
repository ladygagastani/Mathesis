# Word studies II: mind, city and two verbs

Six more words: two for the inner life, one for the community, one for value, and the two
verbs you cannot read a page without. Page 7 explains the terms and the ✓ / ? marks.

---

## ψυχή *psykhē*: breath, life, soul

**Dictionary form:** ψυχή, -ῆς, ἡ, "life, soul, spirit; ghost"

**Why this word.** No Greek word changed its meaning more. It began as the breath that
leaves a dying body, and became the self, the soul, and in the end the "psyche" of modern
psychology. Its sound also shows two of the changes from page 3.

**Where it comes from: ? uncertain beyond Greek.** Within Greek it belongs with the verb
ψύχω *psykhō* "breathe, blow; cool", so the earliest sense was "breath". Comparisons outside
Greek have been suggested, but none is established.

### Through the eras
- **Homeric:** the ψυχή is what a person *loses*: the life-breath that leaves at death and
  goes to Hades as a shade. In the *Iliad*'s third line the ψυχαί of the heroes go down to
  Hades, while "they themselves" (αὐτούς), meaning their bodies, are left for the dogs. For
  Homer, the person is the body; the ψυχή is what departs. In the *Odyssey* it simply means
  "life".
- **Classical:** philosophy turned it inside out. For Plato the ψυχή is the true self:
  immortal, the seat of thought and character, and the body its temporary house.
  Aristotle's treatise *On the Soul* (Περὶ ψυχῆς) makes it whatever makes a living thing
  alive, so that plants and animals have a ψυχή too.
- **Koine and Byzantine:** in the New Testament it is often "life" or "self". In Christian
  Greek it becomes the soul that is saved.
- **Modern:** ψυχή *psichí*, "soul, spirit", and also "courage, heart". In Plato's time the
  word sounded roughly *psü-khḗ*: the υ was rounded like French *u*, and χ was *k* with a
  puff of breath. Today υ is *i* (itacism), and χ before *i* is a soft *ch*, as in German
  *ich*.

### In the texts
> πολλὰς δʼ ἰφθίμους ψυχὰς Ἄϊδι προΐαψεν / ἡρώων, αὐτοὺς δὲ ἑλώρια τεῦχε κύνεσσιν
> "…and sent many strong souls of heroes down to Hades, and made the men themselves a prey
> for dogs."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 1.3–4](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.3))

> ἀρνύμενος ἥν τε ψυχὴν καὶ νόστον ἑταίρων
> "…striving to win his own life and his companions' homecoming."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.5](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.5))

### In English
*psyche*, *psychology*, *psychiatry*, *psychedelic*, *metempsychosis* ("transmigration of
souls").

## λόγος *logos* and λέγω *legō*: account, word, reason; to say

**Dictionary forms:**
- λόγος, -ου, ὁ, "word, speech, account, story, argument, reason"
- λέγω, "say, speak", and earlier "gather, pick out, count"

**Why these words.** λόγος is the most many-sided noun in Greek, and its history is a lesson
in *widening*. Its verb λέγω shows why "say" is one of the hardest verbs to look up.

**Where they come from: ✓ certain.** From the PIE root \*leǵ-, "gather, collect". Latin
*legō* "gather, choose, read" is the cousin. From it come *collect*, *elect*, *legion* and
*lecture*, all through Latin. The path from "gather" to "say" runs through *counting*:
- you pick things out one by one;
- then you *recount* them;
- then you tell.

English has taken a similar path with *tell*, which once meant "count": a bank *teller*,
"all told".

### Through the eras
- **Homeric:** λέγω still often means "pick out, gather, count", though "tell" is there too.
  The noun λόγος is rare in Homer: it appears in the *Odyssey*'s first book, where the nymph
  Calypso keeps Odysseus with "soft and wheedling words" (see below).
- **Classical:** the word explodes in meaning:
  - a word, a speech, a story
  - an account (λόγον διδόναι, "to give an account")
  - an argument, a reckoning, a ratio in mathematics
  - above all, **reason**, the power to give accounts

  Aristotle makes it what distinguishes humans (see below).

  The verb's principal parts show why it is hard to look up: λέγω, **ἐρῶ**, **εἶπον**,
  εἴρηκα. The future and aorist come from quite different roots (page 4).
- **Koine:** the Gospel of John opens with ὁ λόγος, "the Word" (quoted on page 8). How much
  the idea owes to Greek philosophy and how much to Jewish scripture's creative "word of God"
  is much debated.
- **Modern:** λόγος *lóghos* means "speech" (a speech) and "reason, cause": για ποιο λόγο;
  "for what reason?". The everyday word for "word" is now λέξη. "I say" is λέω, a shortened
  form of λέγω.

### In the texts
> αἰεὶ δὲ μαλακοῖσι καὶ αἱμυλίοισι λόγοισιν / θέλγει, ὅπως Ἰθάκης ἐπιλήσεται
> "…and always with soft and wheedling words she charms him, so that he will forget Ithaca."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.56–57](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.56), of Calypso)

> λόγον δὲ μόνον ἄνθρωπος ἔχει τῶν ζῴων
> "Of the animals, man alone has *logos*: speech, and reason."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 1.2, 1253a](read:tlg0086.tlg035:1.1253a))

Aristotle goes on to contrast mere voice (φωνή), which animals have too and which signals
pain and pleasure, with λόγος, which can show what is useful and harmful, just and unjust.

### In English
*logic*, *-logy* (biology, theology…), *dialogue*, *catalogue*, *prologue*, *epilogue*,
*analogy*, *apology* (ἀπολογία, "speech in defence", hence Plato's *Apology*), *lexicon*
(from λέξις "speech, word"), *eclectic* (from ἐκλέγω "pick out").

## πόλις *polis*: city, city-state

**Dictionary form:** πόλις, πόλεως, ἡ, "city, city-state; citadel"

**Why this word.** It shows a meaning narrowing to one particular thing: from "stronghold" to
"city-state" to one single City. The name of a modern metropolis hides it.

**Where it comes from: ✓ probable.** Its cognates are Sanskrit *púr* "fortress, city" and
Lithuanian *pilìs* "castle", so the first sense was "stronghold". The exact PIE form is
debated. Epic poetry also has the older-looking πτόλις *ptolis*, with *pt-*.

### Through the eras
- **Homeric:** a fortified town, often its citadel. Troy is a πόλις, and in the *Odyssey*'s
  second line its "holy citadel" (πτολίεθρον, from the *pt-* form) is sacked. The high
  fortified part of a city is the ἀκρόπολις *akropolis*, "upper city".
- **Classical:** the πόλις becomes the **city-state**: not just a place, but a
  self-governing community of citizens (πολῖται) with its own laws and gods. Aristotle's
  *Politics*, "matters of the polis", argues that humans are made for it (see below).
- **Koine:** after Alexander, new cities such as Alexandria spread Greek life across the
  East. In the New Testament πόλις is simply "town".
- **Byzantine:** Constantinople was so completely *the* city that people called it simply ἡ
  Πόλις, "the City". The Turkish name **Istanbul** most probably comes from the spoken
  Medieval Greek phrase *stin Poli(n)*, "in (or to) the City".
- **Modern:** πόλη *póli*. Nouns in -ις regularly became -η in Modern Greek (δύναμις →
  δύναμη "power"). Greeks still call Istanbul η Πόλη.

### In the texts
> πλάγχθη, ἐπεὶ Τροίης ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον ἔπερσεν
> "…who wandered far, after he had sacked the holy citadel of Troy."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.2](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.2))

> ἐκ τούτων οὖν φανερὸν ὅτι τῶν φύσει ἡ πόλις ἐστί, καὶ ὅτι ὁ ἄνθρωπος φύσει πολιτικὸν ζῷον
> "From this it is clear that the city exists by nature, and that man is by nature an
> animal of the polis."
> ([Aristotle, *Politics* 1.2, 1253a](read:tlg0086.tlg035:1.1253a))

(πολιτικὸν ζῷον is usually translated "political animal". Aristotle means a creature made to
live in a πόλις.)

### In English
*politics*, *policy*, *police* (from πολιτεία, "citizenship, constitution", through Latin and
French), *metropolis*, *cosmopolitan* (κοσμοπολίτης, "citizen of the world"), *necropolis*,
and place names from *Naples* (Νεάπολις, "new city") to *Minneapolis*.

## καλός *kalos*: beautiful, fine, good

**Dictionary form:** καλός, -ή, -όν, "beautiful; fine, noble"

**Why this word.** It shows a meaning *shifting*: the Greek word for "beautiful" became the
Modern Greek word for "good", and the word for "beautiful" is now a different one.

**Where it comes from: ? probable at best.** Early Greek had *kalwos*, with the lost *w*
seen in οἶκος. Homer's scansion still shows it (below). A connection with Sanskrit
*kalyāṇa-* "beautiful, excellent" is often suggested, but the deeper origin is uncertain.

### Through the eras
- **Homeric:** "beautiful, fine", of people, clothes, weapons, a harbour. The first syllable
  usually counts as *long* in Homer's verse (κᾱλός), a trace of the lost *w*.
- **Classical:** beauty and moral excellence merge. τὸ καλόν is "the fine, the noble", what
  is admirable in conduct as well as in looks. The Athenian ideal gentleman was καλὸς
  κἀγαθός, "fine and good" (κἀγαθός is καὶ ἀγαθός run together, page 3). Athenian painted
  vases often carry the words "Λέαγρος καλός", "Leagros is beautiful", praise of a
  fashionable young man.
- **Koine:** in the Septuagint's creation story God sees each thing "that it was καλόν"
  (below). The translators used καλός for the Hebrew word for "good", a step towards the
  modern meaning.
- **Modern:** καλός *kalós* is the ordinary word for "good":
  - καλημέρα "good morning"
  - καλό ταξίδι "have a good trip"

  "Beautiful" is now ωραίος (from ὡραῖος, "in season, in its prime", from ὥρα "season,
  hour") or όμορφος (from εὔμορφος, "well-shaped").

### In the texts
> καὶ ἴδεν ὁ θεὸς τὸ φῶς ὅτι καλόν
> "And God saw the light, that it was good."
> ([Genesis 1:4](read:tlg0527.tlg001:1.4), Septuagint. On the spelling ἴδεν, see page 3.)

### In English
*calligraphy* (from κάλλος "beauty": "beautiful writing"), *kaleidoscope* (coined in 1817 by
David Brewster from καλός, εἶδος "form" and σκοπέω "look at"), *calisthenics* (κάλλος +
σθένος "strength").

## ἔχω *ekhō*: have, hold

**Dictionary form:** ἔχω, "have, hold; (with an adverb) be in a certain state"

**Principal parts:** ἔχω, ἕξω (or σχήσω), ἔσχον, ἔσχηκα. The imperfect is εἶχον.

**Why this word.** It is one of the commonest verbs, and it is written in Linear B and in
today's Athens in the same way. Its principal parts also contain a hidden story of sound
change: why ἔχω has a smooth breathing but ἕξω a rough one.

**Where it comes from: ✓ certain.** From the PIE root \*seǵʰ-, "hold, overpower". Its
cognates include Sanskrit *sáhate* "overcomes, endures" and Gothic *sigis* (German *Sieg*)
"victory".

### The story in the spelling
1. **The *s* became *h*.** An *s* at the start of a word became *h* in Greek (page 7), so
   the root began *hekh-*.
2. **Grassmann's law.** Greek did not tolerate two "breathy" sounds in neighbouring
   syllables: the first lost its breath. *hekhō* has *h* and *kh*, so it became ἔχω, with
   the smooth breathing.
3. **The future keeps the *h*.** In the future the χ runs into σ and becomes plain ξ, with no
   breath. Nothing is left to clash with, so the *h* survives: ἕξω.
4. **The other forms.** The aorist ἔσχον keeps the original *s* (protected inside the word).
   The imperfect εἶχον comes from *e-hekh-on*, contracted.

Three spellings of one root, all explained by rule. English *hectic* (from ἑκτικός
"habitual", related to ἕξις "state") still carries the *h*.

### Through the eras
- **Mycenaean:** *e-ke*, "holds", is one of the commonest words in the land records from
  Pylos. It appears in entries recording who holds which plot, including a dispute over land
  held by the priestess Eritha.
- **Homeric and Classical:** "have, hold, keep". With an adverb it means "to be" in some
  state:
  - καλῶς ἔχει "it is well"
  - πῶς ἔχεις; "how are you?" (literally "how do you hold?")

  With an infinitive it means "to be able": οὐκ ἔχω εἰπεῖν "I cannot say".
- **Modern:** έχω *écho*, unchanged in spelling. Τι έχεις; means "What's wrong with you?".
  The verb now also forms Modern Greek's perfect tense: έχω γράψει, "I have written", built
  like the English.

### In the texts
> ἔχει δέ τε κίονας αὐτὸς / μακράς, αἳ γαῖάν τε καὶ οὐρανὸν ἀμφὶς ἔχουσιν
> "…and he himself holds the tall pillars that keep earth and sky apart."\*
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.53–54](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.53), of Atlas)

Aristotle's λόγον δὲ μόνον ἄνθρωπος **ἔχει** τῶν ζῴων (quoted under λόγος) uses it too:
"man alone *has* logos".

### In English
*scheme* (σχῆμα "form, bearing", from the σχ- stem), *epoch* (ἐποχή "a check, a halt", from
ἐπέχω "hold back"), *hectic*, *eunuch* (εὐνοῦχος "bed-keeper").

## εἰμί *eimi*: be

**Dictionary form:** εἰμί, "be, exist". Future ἔσομαι, imperfect ἦν. It has no aorist or
perfect of its own; Greek uses γίγνομαι "become" to fill the gap.

**Why this word.** It is the commonest verb of all, and the best example of a word shared by
the whole Indo-European family: *is* and ἐστί are the same word. In Modern Greek it took on
new endings.

**Where it comes from: ✓ certain.** From the PIE root \*h₁es-, "be". Compare:

| Language | "I am" | "is" |
|---|---|---|
| Greek | εἰμί | ἐστί |
| Sanskrit | *ásmi* | *ásti* |
| Latin | *sum* | *est* |
| English | *am* | *is* |
| German | | *ist* |

εἰμί comes from \*es-mi. The *s* dropped out before *m* and the vowel was lengthened in
compensation, which is why it is spelled ει.

### Through the eras
- **Homeric:** many old forms. The infinitive is often ἔμμεναι, where Attic has εἶναι. You
  met it in Telemachus' line on page 8.
- **Classical:** the forms matter for meaning. When ἐστί means "is" joining two things
  ("Socrates is wise"), it is an enclitic that leans on the word before it. Accented on the
  first syllable, ἔστι means "exists" or "it is possible". Philosophers built on the
  participle ὤν, ὄντος, "being": τὰ ὄντα means "the things that are".
- **Koine:** John's "In the beginning *was* the Word" (page 8) uses the imperfect ἦν three
  times. Koine also began giving the verb middle endings (like -μαι) in some forms.
- **Modern:** είμαι, είσαι, είναι, είμαστε, είστε, είναι. The endings now look like those of
  the middle voice (compare λύομαι "I am loosened"). The ancient active forms are gone.

### In the texts
> φησὶ γάρ που πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἄνθρωπον εἶναι, τῶν μὲν ὄντων ὡς ἔστι, τῶν δὲ μὴ ὄντων ὡς οὐκ ἔστιν
> "For he says, I think, that man is the measure of all things: of the things that are, that
> they are, and of the things that are not, that they are not."\*
> ([Plato, *Theaetetus* 152a](read:tlg0059.tlg006:152a), quoting Protagoras)

Three forms of εἰμί in one sentence:
- εἶναι "to be"
- ὄντων "(of the things) being"
- ἔστι "exists", accented on the first syllable because here it means "exists"

### In English
*ontology* ("the study of being", from ὄν, ὄντος). Latin's cousin verb *esse* gives *essence*,
*present* and *absent*, which are cousins of εἰμί, not borrowings from it.

## Practice

**1. Which of the twelve words is behind each English word?**

economy · psychology · metropolis · tripod · dehydrate · enthusiasm · dialogue · police ·
kaleidoscope · scheme · ontology · philanthropy

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- economy: οἶκος
- psychology: ψυχή
- metropolis: μήτηρ + πόλις
- tripod: πούς
- dehydrate: ὕδωρ
- enthusiasm: θεός
- dialogue: λόγος / λέγω
- police: πόλις
- kaleidoscope: καλός
- scheme: ἔχω
- ontology: εἰμί
- philanthropy: ἄνθρωπος
</details>

**2. Name the kind of change** (sound change, widening, narrowing, shift, replacement,
survival, rebuilt form).

(a) πούς → Modern πόδι · (b) ὕδωρ → Modern νερό · (c) οἶκος → Modern σπίτι · (d) καλός
"beautiful" → Modern "good" · (e) πόλις → "the City" (Constantinople) · (f) θεός → Modern θεός
· (g) μήτηρ → Modern μητέρα · (h) λόγος "account" → "word, speech, reason" · (i) Linear B
*a-to-ro-qo* → ἄνθρωπος

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- (a) Replacement, by a diminutive.
- (b) Replacement, by a worn-down phrase (νεαρὸν ὕδωρ "fresh water").
- (c) Replacement, by a loanword from Latin.
- (d) Shift.
- (e) Narrowing, or specialisation.
- (f) Survival.
- (g) Rebuilt form: the accusative became the nominative.
- (h) Widening.
- (i) Sound change: *kʷ* became π.
</details>

**3. Find the dictionary form.**

ποδός · οἴκῳ · ὕδατος · ψυχάς · μητρός · πόλεως · ἕξω · ὄντων · ἀνθρώπου · ἦν

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- ποδός → πούς (genitive singular)
- οἴκῳ → οἶκος (dative singular)
- ὕδατος → ὕδωρ (genitive singular)
- ψυχάς → ψυχή (accusative plural)
- μητρός → μήτηρ (genitive singular)
- πόλεως → πόλις (genitive singular)
- ἕξω → ἔχω (future: "I shall have")
- ὄντων → εἰμί (participle, genitive plural)
- ἀνθρώπου → ἄνθρωπος (genitive singular)
- ἦν → εἰμί (imperfect: "was")
</details>

**4. Cousin or borrowing?** Is each English word a *cognate* of the Greek (descended from
the same ancestor) or a *borrowing* from it?

*water* / ὕδωρ · *hydrant* / ὕδωρ · *foot* / πούς · *tripod* / πούς · *mother* / μήτηρ ·
*metropolis* / μήτηρ · *is* / ἐστί · and the odd one out, Latin *deus* / θεός

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- *water*, *foot*, *mother* and *is* are cognates: cousins of the Greek words.
- *hydrant*, *tripod* and *metropolis* are borrowings, built from Greek.
- *deus* and θεός are neither: they only look alike. *Deus* is related to Ζεύς.
</details>

## For review (not for publication)
- **ψυχή.** The ψύχω connection is the usual one; the page does not claim a PIE root. "Courage, heart" as a Modern sense comes from Wiktionary. The Classical summary of Plato and Aristotle is general and uncontroversial, with no quotations.
- **λόγος in Homer.** I believe it occurs only twice (*Iliad* 15.393 and *Odyssey* 1.56), and the page says just "rare". Please confirm with a concordance. The "tell = count" parallel is standard English etymology. The John 1:1 aside is deliberately neutral.
- **λέγω's principal parts.** Only four are given. The full Attic set is λέγω, ἐρῶ / λέξω, εἶπον / ἔλεξα, εἴρηκα, εἴρημαι / λέλεγμαι, ἐλέχθην / ἐρρήθην.
- **πόλις.** The etymology is marked "probable" because Wiktionary gives \*tpólHis with Sanskrit and Lithuanian cognates, and some handbooks are more cautious. On **Istanbul**, Wikipedia's *Names of Istanbul* cites a 2015 review favouring the colloquial Medieval Greek στην Πόλι(ν) over the older εἰς τὴν πόλιν. The page says "most probably".
- **καλός.** The *kalwos* form and the long first syllable in Homer are from Wiktionary; that the Boeotian inscriptions actually write ΚΑΛϜΟΣ is worth confirming. The Sanskrit comparison is flagged as uncertain. The Leagros *kalos* inscriptions are well known, but a museum example could be linked. Brewster's 1817 coinage is standard.
- **ἔχω.** *e-ke* is confirmed on Wiktionary, and the Eritha dispute (PY Ep 704) via Wikipedia's article on Eritha. The Grassmann's-law explanation is textbook. "Commonest words in the land records" is an impression, not a count. Soften it if you prefer.
- **εἰμί.** I left out any Linear B form: *e-e-si* (for later εἰσί, Homeric ἔασι) is often cited, but I could not confirm it online, so check it in *DMic* before adding. "Koine also began giving the verb middle endings" rests on forms like ἤμην "I was", found in the New Testament; confirm which forms you would like to cite.
- **Aristotle references.** *Politics* 1.2, 1253a2–3 and 1253a9–10: the Bekker line numbers come from the milestones in the app's text.
