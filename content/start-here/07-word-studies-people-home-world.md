# Word studies I: people, home and world

Six words for the most basic things: a person, a mother, a foot, a house, water and a god.

Step 6 explains the terms, and the ✓ and **?** marks.

---

## ἄνθρωπος *anthrōpos*: human being

**Dictionary form:** ἄνθρωπος, -ου, ὁ, "human being, person"

**Why this word.** One of the commonest nouns in Greek, spelled the same since Homer. Its
oldest spelling shows a sound Greek later lost, and it teaches the difference from ἀνήρ,
"man".

**Where it comes from: ? uncertain.** No one agrees. It may be a compound of ἀνήρ "man" and
ὤψ "face" ("man-faced"), though the sounds do not fit well, or a borrowing from a language
spoken in the Aegean before Greek arrived. Neither is proven.

### Through the eras
- **Mycenaean:** written *a-to-ro-qo*. By the Linear B spelling rules (step 6) this
  stands for *anthrōkʷos*, with the rounded *kʷ* sound. Later that sound became π before ο,
  so *-kʷos* became -πος.
- **Homeric:** ἄνθρωποι are "mortals", humans as opposed to gods. The heroes themselves are
  more often ἄνδρες, "men".
- **Classical:** ἄνθρωπος is the human being in general, woman or man; ἀνήρ is the adult
  male, husband or hero. In conversation ὦ ἄνθρωπε can be a brusque "my good man!".
- **Koine:** the Gospels' phrase ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, "the Son of Man", made the word
  central to Christian Greek.
- **Modern:** άνθρωπος, still "person, human being". It is spelled the same (with the single
  modern accent mark) and pronounced *ánthropos*, with θ as in *thin*.

### In the texts
> πολλῶν δʼ ἀνθρώπων ἴδεν ἄστεα καὶ νόον ἔγνω
> "Many were the men whose cities he saw, and whose mind he learned."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.3](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.3))

> ὅτι πολλοὶ μὲν ἄνθρωποι εἶεν, ὀλίγοι δὲ ἄνδρες
> "…that they were many human beings, but few men."
> ([Herodotus 7.210](read:tlg0016.tlg001:7.210), of the Persian army at Thermopylae)

> ἦλθεν γὰρ ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ζητῆσαι καὶ σῶσαι τὸ ἀπολωλός
> "For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost."
> ([Luke 19:10](read:tlg0031.tlg003:19.10))

### In English
*anthropology* ("the study of humans"), *philanthropy* (φιλανθρωπία, "love of humankind"),
*misanthrope*, *anthropomorphic* ("in human shape").

## μήτηρ *mētēr*: mother

**Dictionary form:** μήτηρ, μητρός, ἡ, "mother"

**Why this word.** Few words are so securely traced to Proto-Indo-European. It also shows the
main sound difference between the dialects, and how Modern Greek remade irregular nouns.

**Where it comes from: ✓ certain.** From PIE \*méh₂tēr, "mother". Its cognates include:
- Latin *māter*
- Sanskrit *mātár-*
- Old Church Slavonic *mati*
- Old English *mōdor*, which became English *mother*

### Through the eras
- **Mycenaean:** *ma-te-re*, "to the mother", on a single tablet from Pylos. It records
  perfumed oil for *ma-te-re te-i-ja*, "the Divine Mother". Who she was is debated: a
  goddess, perhaps one borrowed from Anatolia, or even a woman of the palace.
- **The dialects:** early Greek said *mātēr*, with a long *ā*. Doric kept it (μάτηρ), but
  Attic and Ionic changed long *ā* to η (*ē*): μήτηρ. This one change explains many
  dialect pairs:
  - δᾶμος and δῆμος "people" (Linear B *da-mo*)
  - ἁμέρα and ἡμέρα "day"

  Pindar, writing in a literary Doric, has ἐν ἁμέρᾳ "in the day" in the sixth line of his
  first *Olympian*.
- **Homeric:** in the *Odyssey*, the young Telemachus answers a stranger who asks whether he
  is Odysseus' son (see the quotation below).
- **Classical:** the word declines unevenly: μήτηρ, μητρός, μητρί, but μητέρα. A colony
  called the city that founded it its μητρόπολις *mētropolis*, "mother-city".
- **Modern:** μητέρα *mitéra*. The old accusative μητέρα has become the ordinary nominative,
  so the noun now declines like any regular noun in -α. πατήρ "father" went the same way
  (Modern πατέρας). In everyday speech "mum" is μάνα or μαμά.

### In the texts
> μήτηρ μέν τέ μέ φησι τοῦ ἔμμεναι, αὐτὰρ ἐγώ γε / οὐκ οἶδʼ
> "My mother says I am his; but I myself do not know."
> ([Homer, *Odyssey* 1.215–16](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.215))

(ἔμμεναι is the Homeric form of εἶναι, "to be": see εἰμί in step 8.)

### In English
*metropolis*, *metropolitan* (from μητρόπολις). English *mother* is a cognate, a cousin of
μήτηρ, not borrowed from it. *Maternal* and *matrix* come from the Latin cousin *māter*.

## πούς *pous*: foot

**Dictionary form:** πούς, ποδός, ὁ, "foot"

**Why this word.** Beside Latin *pēs* and English *foot*, it is the textbook example of
regular sound change. In Modern Greek it was replaced by its own "little" form.

**Where it comes from: ✓ certain.** From PIE \*pṓds, with the stem \*ped- in other cases.
Its cognates include Latin *pēs, pedis*, Sanskrit *pád-*, Gothic *fōtus* and English *foot*.
English shows Grimm's law at work: *p* → *f*, *d* → *t*. The Greek genitive ποδός shows the
stem ποδ-. The nominative πούς is irregular even within Greek, and its vowel is an old
puzzle.

### Through the eras
- **Mycenaean:** the word appears inside compounds, most famously *ti-ri-po*, *tripos*,
  "three-footed", a tripod cauldron. A tablet from Pylos (PY Ta 641) lists tripods and other
  vessels, each beside a small drawing of the vessel. When Ventris's readings of the words
  matched the drawings, this helped confirm the decipherment.
- **Homeric:** Achilles' stock epithet is πόδας ὠκύς, "swift-footed". Literally it means
  "swift as to his feet": the accusative πόδας says *in what respect* he is swift.
- **Classical:** a unit of length, and in verse the **metrical foot**, the unit of rhythm.
  Latin took the idea over as *pes*, which is why English poetry, too, has "feet".
- **Koine and Byzantine:** a diminutive, πόδιον "little foot", gradually lost its smallness
  and became the ordinary word. Medieval Greek had πόδιν.
- **Modern:** πόδι *pódi* "foot, leg". Many everyday Modern Greek nouns began as
  diminutives in -ιον in the same way. The old word lives on in learned compounds such as
  τρίποδο "tripod".

### In the texts
> τοῖσι δʼ ἀνιστάμενος μετέφη πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς
> "Then, standing up among them, spoke swift-footed Achilles."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 1.58](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.58))

### In English
*tripod*, *octopus* (ὀκτώπους, "eight-foot"), *antipodes* (ἀντίποδες, people "with feet
opposite" ours), *podium* (via Latin, from πόδιον), *gastropod*, *podiatry*. The name
*Oedipus* (Οἰδίπους) was explained in antiquity as "swollen foot".

## οἶκος *oikos*: house, household

**Dictionary form:** οἶκος, -ου, ὁ, "house, household, family estate"

**Why this word.** It remembers a lost letter. Everyday Modern Greek replaced it with a word
borrowed from Latin, yet it gave English one of its largest families of learned words.

**Where it comes from: ✓ certain.** From PIE \*wóyḱos, "settlement". Its cognates include:
- Latin *vīcus*, "village, district of a town"
- Sanskrit *viś-*, "settlement, clan"
- Gothic *weihs*, "village"

English place names in *-wich* and *-wick* (Norwich, Warwick) come from the Latin cousin
*vīcus*, borrowed into Old English.

### Through the eras
- **Mycenaean:** *wo-ko*, *woikos*. The initial *w* was written in some later Greek
  alphabets with the letter **digamma** (Ϝ), so called because it looks like one gamma on
  top of another. In Attic and Ionic the sound was lost, and the word became οἶκος.
- **Homeric:** the *w* was gone from speech by Homer's time, but the verse often behaves as
  though it were still there: a vowel before οἶκος is often not dropped, as it would be
  before a true vowel. One of many signs that the epic language is very old.
- **Classical:** the οἶκος is the household, not just the building: family, slaves, land and
  goods, the basic unit of the city. Running it was οἰκονομία *oikonomia*, "household
  management", the subject of Xenophon's dialogue the *Oeconomicus*.
- **Koine:** in the Gospels, the household is the family. Christian Greek then built new
  words from the stem:
  - ἡ οἰκουμένη, "the inhabited (world)"
  - παροικία, "sojourn in a foreign land", which became Latin *parochia* and English *parish*
  - διοίκησις, "administration", which became *diocese*
- **Byzantine and Modern:** the everyday word for house became σπίτι *spíti*, from Medieval
  σπίτιν and earlier ὁσπίτιον, a borrowing of Latin *hospitium* "lodging". Formal Modern
  Greek still uses οίκος: εκδοτικός οίκος "publishing house", and in compounds like
  οικογένεια "family" and οικονομία "economy".

### In the texts
> σήμερον σωτηρία τῷ οἴκῳ τούτῳ ἐγένετο
> "Today salvation has come to this house."
> ([Luke 19:9](read:tlg0031.tlg003:19.9), Jesus to the tax-collector Zacchaeus)

(τῷ οἴκῳ is the dative, "to the house". Note the iota subscript.)

### In English
*economy*, *economics*, *ecology* (German *Ökologie*, coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866),
*ecosystem*, *ecumenical*, *parish*, *diocese*.

## ὕδωρ *hydōr*: water

**Dictionary form:** ὕδωρ, ὕδατος, τό, "water"

**Why this word.** Its English cousin is obvious: *water*. Everyday Greek replaced it with a
word that once meant "fresh", but it survives in every English *hydro-* word.

**Where it comes from: ✓ certain.** From PIE \*wódr̥, genitive \*udn-és. Its cognates
include:
- Hittite *wātar*
- Sanskrit *udán-*
- Old English *wæter* (English *water*)
- Russian *voda*, whose diminutive "little water" is *vodka*
- Latin *unda*, "wave"

The PIE word had *r* in the nominative and *n* in the other cases. Greek turned that *n*
into α, which is why the genitive is ὕδ**α**τος. The rough breathing follows the rule for
initial υ (step 3).

### Through the eras
- **Classical:** Pindar begins his first *Olympian* ode, written for Hieron of Syracuse,
  with water, ranking it above even gold (see the quotation below).
- **Koine:** the Septuagint uses the genitive in the second verse of the Bible (see below).
- **Byzantine:** people spoke of νεαρὸν ὕδωρ, "fresh water". The adjective came to stand
  for the whole phrase, the way English says "a daily" for a daily newspaper. Koine νηρόν
  became Medieval νερό(ν).
- **Modern:** νερό *neró* "water". ύδωρ survives only in formal and technical Greek:
  υδραυλικός, literally "hydraulic", is the everyday word for "plumber".

### In the texts
> ἄριστον μὲν ὕδωρ, ὁ δὲ χρυσὸς αἰθόμενον πῦρ / ἅτε διαπρέπει νυκτὶ μεγάνορος ἔξοχα πλούτου
> "Best is water; and gold, like blazing fire in the night, shines out above all lordly
> wealth."
> ([Pindar, *Olympian* 1.1–2](read:tlg0033.tlg001:1.1))

> καὶ πνεῦμα θεοῦ ἐπεφέρετο ἐπάνω τοῦ ὕδατος
> "And the spirit of God was moving over the water."
> ([Genesis 1:2](read:tlg0527.tlg001:1.2), Septuagint)

### In English
*hydraulic*, *hydrant*, *dehydrate*, *hydrogen* (French *hydrogène*, coined in the 1780s,
"water-maker"), *Hydra*. Among the cognates, English *otter* (the "water animal") and Greek
ὕδρα "water-snake" are also related.

## θεός *theos*: god

**Dictionary form:** θεός, -οῦ, ὁ or ἡ, "god, goddess"

**Why this word.** Almost nothing about it has changed in 3,300 years, and it is the best
warning in Greek against trusting look-alikes.

**Where it comes from: ? uncertain.** The early Greek form was \*thehos (hence Linear B
*te-o*). Its deeper origin is disputed. One proposal connects it with a PIE root found in
Latin *fānum* "shrine" and *fēriae* "holy days", but no explanation is agreed.

One thing *is* certain. θεός is **not** related to Latin *deus* "god", although they look
and mean alike. *Deus* belongs with Greek Ζεύς (genitive Διός) and Latin *diēs* "day", from
a PIE word for the bright sky. The resemblance is a coincidence, like English *have* and
Latin *habēre*, which are also unrelated.

### Through the eras
- **Mycenaean:** *te-o*, "god". The tablets record offerings to gods, several of them with
  names still familiar later: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes.
- **Homeric:** the feminine θεά "goddess" is the third word of the *Iliad*.
- **Classical:** in Attic prose ἡ θεός, with the feminine article, is "the goddess", often
  meaning Athena. When Socrates in the *Apology* speaks of ὁ θεός, he means Apollo of
  Delphi.
- **Koine:** the opening of John's Gospel (see below). θεός comes first in its clause, for
  emphasis, and has no article, which marks it as the predicate (what is said *about* ὁ
  λόγος). The exact force of this has been discussed since antiquity.
- **Byzantine:** a flood of compounds. Θεοτόκος "God-bearer", the title of Mary affirmed at
  the Council of Ephesus (431), is one of the best known.
- **Modern:** θεός *theós*, pronounced with θ as in *thin* but otherwise unchanged: Θεέ μου,
  "my God!".

### In the texts
> μῆνιν ἄειδε θεὰ Πηληϊάδεω Ἀχιλῆος
> "Sing, goddess, the wrath of Peleus' son Achilles."
> ([Homer, *Iliad* 1.1](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.1))

> ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος
> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."
> ([John 1:1](read:tlg0031.tlg004:1.1))

### In English
*theology*, *atheist*, *theism*, *pantheon*, *apotheosis*, *enthusiasm* (ἐνθουσιασμός, from
ἔνθεος "with a god inside"), and the names *Theodore* ("god's gift"), *Dorothy* (the same
parts reversed) and *Timothy* ("honouring god").

## For review (not for publication)
- **Shortened (25 Sep 2026):** the "Why this word" paragraphs and the ἄνθρωπος etymology were tightened (same claims, fewer words); "In Greek of the first millennium BCE that sound became π" is now "Later that sound became π"; the page references now read "step N".
- **ἄνθρωπος.** Its etymologies are summarized from Wiktionary, which reports the ἀνήρ + ὤψ proposal (defended by Rosén), Beekes' pre-Greek view, and Garnier's PIE proposal. Worth checking against Beekes' *Etymological Dictionary of Greek* and Chantraine.
- **Where *a-to-ro-qo* is attested.** I have not stated which site it comes from. I believe Pylos; please confirm in *DMic* or Ventris & Chadwick.
- ***ma-te-re te-i-ja*.** PY Fr 1202 is its only attestation. The phrasing "perhaps one borrowed from Anatolia, or even a woman of the palace" reflects the range of views in the literature I found, which includes a UNC thesis questioning the goddess reading.
- **πούς.** The "old puzzle" of the nominative vowel: Doric πώς is attested, and the Attic ου is discussed in the handbooks. I kept it vague on purpose. I also did not claim a Linear B form of the simple noun. Wiktionary gives a starred \*po, which suggests it is not straightforwardly attested.
- **"Many everyday Modern Greek nouns began as diminutives in -ιον."** I deliberately gave no examples. The standard ones are μάτι < ὀμμάτιον and ψωμί < ψωμίον; add them if you confirm them.
- **Digamma inscriptions.** The claim that ϝοῖκος is attested in dialect inscriptions comes from Wiktionary. The page says only that the digamma was written "in some later Greek alphabets". The Homeric hiatus claim is standard; an example line could be added.
- **ὕδωρ.** I found no secure Linear B form, so there is no Mycenaean entry. The *otter*/ὕδρα connection is standard but not essential to the study. The "fresh water" etymology of νερό is from Wiktionary and is widely repeated. Chantraine or Andriotis would be the formal source.
- **θεός.** Its etymology is genuinely disputed. The *fānum*/*fēriae* link follows Wiktionary's \*dʰeh₁s- analysis, so check it against Beekes. The *have*/*habēre* comparison is a standard textbook example of false cognates.
- **Mycenaean gods.** Zeus, Hera, Poseidon and Hermes are all in the list of Mycenaean deities (Wikipedia). A handbook citation would be better.
- **John 1:1** is quoted in lowercase. The app's edition prints the opening words in capitals (ΕΝ ΑΡΧΗ).
- **Dates.** "3,300 years" for θεός assumes Linear B began c. 1400–1300 BCE. The Council of Ephesus (431) and Haeckel's coinage (1866) are standard dates.
