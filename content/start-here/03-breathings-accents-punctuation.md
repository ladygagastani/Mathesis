# Breathings, accents and punctuation

Printed Greek is covered in small marks. They look alarming but are quick to learn:
breathings (is there an *h*?), accents (which syllable rises) and punctuation. The page ends
with how the sound of Greek changed between Plato and today.

## Breathings: is there an *h*?

Every word that **begins with a vowel** (or with ρ) carries a **breathing** mark:

| Mark | Name | Say | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ῾ (like a small *c*) | rough breathing | *h* before the vowel | ὅρος *horos* "boundary" |
| ᾿ (like an apostrophe) | smooth breathing | nothing extra | ὄρος *oros* "mountain" |

As the example shows, the breathing alone can tell two words apart.

Three things to know:
1. **Where the mark goes.** On a small letter it sits on top: ἁ. On a capital it sits to the
   left: Ὅμηρος *Homēros* "Homer". On a diphthong it sits on the second vowel: αὐτός *autos*.
2. **Initial υ always has the rough breathing** (in Attic): ὕδωρ *hydōr* "water", ὑπό *hypo*
   "under". That is why English has so many words that begin *hy-*: *hydraulic*, *hypothesis*.
3. **Initial ρ always has the rough breathing:** ῥήτωρ *rhētōr* "public speaker" gives
   *rhetoric*. The *rh* spelling of English *rhythm* and *rhinoceros* comes from this.

There is no letter for *h* in the classical alphabet. Athens once used Η for the sound *h*,
but gave that up when it adopted the Ionic alphabet (in 403 BCE, officially), in which Η
is the vowel η. The breathing signs were added centuries later by scholars in Alexandria, to
help readers of old poetry. The rough-breathing sign began as the left half of Η.

## Accents: the music of the voice

Most Greek words carry one of three **accent** marks:

| Accent | Mark | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **acute** | ά | λόγος *logos* "word" |
| **grave** | ὰ | θεὸς ἦν "was God" |
| **circumflex** | ᾶ | οἶκος *oikos* "house" |

In English we make the accented syllable *louder* (*PHO-to-graph*). In Ancient Greek it
was said on a *higher note*, like singing it slightly higher. An ancient critic, Dionysius
of Halicarnassus, says the voice rose by about a musical fifth.

- **Acute** ( ´ ): the voice rises on this syllable.
- **Circumflex** ( ῀ ): the voice rises and falls on this one syllable. It needs room, so it
  sits only on a long vowel or a diphthong, which makes it a clue to length: μῆνιν *mēnin*
  "wrath" ([*Iliad* 1.1](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.1)).
- **Grave** ( ` ): an acute on a word's last syllable turns into a grave when another word
  follows straight on. θεός alone, but καὶ **θεὸς** ἦν ὁ λόγος, "and the Word was God"
  ([John 1:1](read:tlg0031.tlg004:1.1)). It probably means the voice did *not* rise.

Two rules help you read. The accent can stand only on one of a word's **last three**
syllables. The circumflex can stand only on one of the **last two**.

**How to say them today.** Raise your voice slightly on the accented syllable, or simply
stress it, as most readers do. Either way, keep long vowels long.

**Why accents are worth reading:** they tell apart words that are otherwise spelled alike.
- τίς *tis* "who?" and τις *tis* "someone"
- ποῦ *pou* "where?" and που *pou* "somewhere"
- νόμος *nomos* "law" and νομός *nomos* "pasture, district"
- εἰμί *eimi* "I am" and εἶμι *eimi* "I shall go"

Words like τις and που usually have no accent of their own. They lean on the word before
them, so they are called **enclitics** ("leaning" words).

Accents were first written by scholars at Alexandria. Tradition credits Aristophanes of
Byzantium, around 200 BCE. They came into regular use only in the medieval manuscripts
from which our printed texts descend.

## Punctuation and other marks

| Mark | Name | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **;** | question mark | Looks like an English semicolon: τίς εἶ; "who are you?" |
| **·** | raised dot (*ano teleia*) | A pause stronger than a comma: the work of our colon or semicolon |
| **, .** | comma, full stop | As in English |
| **ʼ** | apostrophe | A vowel has been dropped before another vowel (**elision**): πολλὰς δʼ = πολλὰς δέ ([*Iliad* 1.3](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.3)) |
| **᾽** over a merged word | coronis | Two words have run together (**crasis**): κἀγώ = καὶ ἐγώ "and I" |

Ancient books had almost no punctuation and no spaces between words. Everything you see
was added by modern editors, and editors sometimes disagree. Most editions capitalize names
and the first word of a paragraph, but not the first word of every sentence. Many print no
quotation marks.

## How the sounds changed

The spelling of Greek has hardly changed since Plato; the sounds have. The main changes,
from Plato's Greek to Modern Greek:

| Letters | In Plato's time | Today | When it changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| θ, φ, χ | *t*, *p*, *k* with a puff | *th* (thin), *f*, *ch* (loch) | early centuries CE |
| β, γ, δ | *b*, *g*, *d* | *v*, a soft *g*, *th* (this) | Roman period |
| ο / ω, ε / η | short / long | same length | late Hellenistic and Roman periods |
| accents | a rise in pitch | a louder syllable | by the early centuries CE |
| η, ι, υ, ει, οι, υι | six different sounds | all *ee* (**itacism**) | in stages, complete by about the 10th century |
| αι | *ai* in "aisle" | *e* in "pet" | Roman period |

**Itacism** (from ἦτα, the name of the letter η) is the change you will notice most. It
explains:
- why Modern Greek pronounces ἡ, οἱ, εἰ and ἤ all as *i*;
- why scribes, spelling by ear, often confused ἡμεῖς "we" and ὑμεῖς "you". This is a classic
  source of the *textual variants* described in the app's Wiki. Modern Greek eventually
  replaced both words, with εμείς and εσείς.

You can see it in the texts you read here. The app's Septuagint text of Genesis 1:4 prints
καὶ **ἴδεν** ὁ θεὸς τὸ φῶς ὅτι καλόν ("and God saw the light, that it was good"). Classical
spelling would be **εἶδεν**, but the scribe wrote ι for ει, because by then they sounded the
same.

## Practice

**1. Transliterate, remembering the breathings.**

ὕδωρ · ἱππόδρομος · Ἑλλάς · ῥυθμός · ἀρχή · ὁρίζων · ὀρφανός

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- *hydōr* (water)
- *hippodromos* (horse-racecourse): *hippodrome*
- *Hellas* (Greece)
- *rhythmos*: *rhythm*
- *arkhē* (beginning, rule): *archaic*, *monarchy*
- *horizōn* (bounding): *horizon*
- *orphanos*: *orphan*. Smooth breathing, so no *h*.
</details>

**2. Why do these words differ?**

(a) ὅρος and ὄρος · (b) τίς; and τις · (c) θεός in ὁ θεός. but θεὸς in θεὸς ἦν

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- (a) The breathing: *horos* "boundary", *oros* "mountain".
- (b) The accent: "who?" (always accented, and here with a question mark) against the
  enclitic "someone".
- (c) Before a full stop the acute stays. When another word follows directly, an acute on
  the last syllable becomes a grave. It is the same word.
</details>

**3. An argument about one letter.** During the plague at Athens (430 BCE), Thucydides says,
people remembered an old verse: "A Dorian war will come, and a λοιμός with it." Some insisted
the verse had really said λιμός ([*History of the Peloponnesian War* 2.54](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.54)). λοιμός means
"plague", λιμός "famine". How did the two words sound in Thucydides' Athens? How do they
sound in Modern Greek?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In Classical Attic they were distinct: *loi-mos* with the diphthong οι, and *lī-mos* with a
long ι. The argument was about a single letter. In Modern Greek, οι and ι have both become
*i*, so the two words sound exactly alike: *limós*.
</details>

## For review (not for publication)
- **Dionysius' "fifth".** This is *On the Arrangement of Words* (*De compositione verborum*) 11. Confirm the section number if it is cited.
- **403 BCE.** The date for Athens' official adoption of the Ionic alphabet is 403/2 BCE, the archonship of Eucleides. It is standard, but it rests on later sources.
- **Aristophanes of Byzantium.** Crediting him with the accents is ancient tradition, not proven fact; the page says "tradition credits". The breathing signs are also Alexandrian, and "the left half of Η" (Ⱶ) is the standard account of their origin.
- **Dates in the sound-change table.** These are broad ranges and vary by region; Egyptian papyri often show changes earlier. The date for η becoming *ee* is especially debated, which is why the table gives only "in stages".
- **ἡμεῖς/ὑμεῖς.** That the confusion *contributed* to Modern εμείς/εσείς is the usual explanation. I did not claim it was the only cause.
- **Genesis 1:4.** The app's First1K text prints ἴδεν. Rahlfs' Septuagint prints εἶδεν. Please confirm against Rahlfs, and check which manuscript the First1K edition follows at this point.
