# Breathings, accents and punctuation

The small marks over the letters, Greek punctuation, and why Greeks today say it all
differently.

## Breathings: is there an *h*?

Every word that **begins with a vowel** (or with ρ) carries a **breathing** mark:

| Mark | Name | Say | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ῾ (like a small *c*) | rough breathing | an *h* before the vowel | ὅρος *horos* "boundary" |
| ᾿ (like an apostrophe) | smooth breathing | nothing extra | ὄρος *oros* "mountain" |

The breathing alone can tell two words apart, as those two do. Three things to know:

1. **Where the mark goes.** On a small letter, on top: ἁ. On a capital, to its left:
   Ὅμηρος *Homēros* "Homer". On a diphthong, on the second vowel: αὐτός *autos*.
2. **A word beginning with υ always has the rough breathing:** ὕδωρ *hydōr* "water", ὑπό
   *hypo* "under". That is why so many English words begin *hy-*: *hydraulic*, *hypothesis*.
3. **A word beginning with ρ always has it too,** and is written *rh*: ῥήτωρ *rhētōr*
   "public speaker" gives *rhetoric*.

Greek has no letter for *h*. The breathing signs were added centuries after Plato, by
scholars in Alexandria, to help readers of old poetry.

## Accents: which syllable rises

Most Greek words carry one of three **accent** marks:

| Accent | Mark | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **acute** | ά | λόγος *logos* "word" |
| **circumflex** | ᾶ | οἶκος *oikos* "house" |
| **grave** | ὰ | θεὸς ἦν "was God" |

In English we make the accented syllable *louder* (*PHO-to-graph*). In Ancient Greek it was
said on a *higher note*. An ancient critic, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, says the voice rose
by about a musical fifth.

- **Acute** ( ´ ): the voice rises on this syllable.
- **Circumflex** ( ῀ ): the voice rises and falls on one syllable. It needs room, so it sits
  only on a long vowel or a diphthong: μῆνιν *mēnin* "wrath"
  ([*Iliad* 1.1](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.1)).
- **Grave** ( ` ): an acute on a word's last syllable turns grave when another word follows
  straight on. θεός alone, but καὶ **θεὸς** ἦν ὁ λόγος, "and the Word was God"
  ([John 1:1](read:tlg0031.tlg004:1.1)). It probably means the voice did *not* rise.

> **How to say them today.** Say the accented syllable a little higher, or simply stress it,
> as most readers do. Either way, keep long vowels long.

**Why accents are worth reading:** they tell apart words that are otherwise spelled alike.

- τίς *tis* "who?" and τις *tis* "someone"
- νόμος *nomos* "law" and νομός *nomos* "pasture, district"
- εἰμί *eimi* "I am" and εἶμι *eimi* "I shall go"

Small words like τις usually have no accent of their own: they lean on the word before them,
and are called **enclitics** ("leaning" words).

## Punctuation

| Mark | Name | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **;** | question mark | Looks like an English semicolon: τίς εἶ; "who are you?" |
| **·** | raised dot | A pause stronger than a comma: our colon or semicolon |
| **, .** | comma, full stop | As in English |
| **ʼ** | apostrophe | A vowel has been dropped before another vowel (**elision**): πολλὰς δʼ = πολλὰς δέ ([*Iliad* 1.3](read:tlg0012.tlg001:1.3)) |
| **᾽** over a merged word | coronis | Two words have run together (**crasis**): κἀγώ = καὶ ἐγώ "and I" |

Ancient books had almost no punctuation and no spaces between words: modern editors added
them. Most editions capitalize names and the first word of a paragraph, but not the first
word of every sentence, and many print no quotation marks.

## How the sounds changed

The spelling of Greek has hardly changed since Plato; the sounds have. The main changes:

| Letters | In Plato's time | Today | When it changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| θ, φ, χ | *t*, *p*, *k* with a puff | "th" in *thin*, "f", "ch" in *loch* | early centuries CE |
| β, γ, δ | "b", "g", "d" | "v", a soft "g", "th" in *this* | Roman period |
| ο / ω, ε / η | short / long | the same length | late Hellenistic and Roman periods |
| accents | a rise in pitch | a louder syllable | by the early centuries CE |
| η, ι, υ, ει, οι, υι | six different sounds | all "ee" (**itacism**) | in stages, complete by about the 10th century |
| αι | "igh" in *sigh* | "e" in *pet* | Roman period |

**Itacism** (from ἦτα, the name of η) is the change you will notice most. Scribes who
spelled by ear often mixed up the letters for "ee". You can see it in the app's Septuagint
text of Genesis 1:4: καὶ **ἴδεν** ὁ θεὸς τὸ φῶς ὅτι καλόν ("and God saw the light, that it
was good"). Classical spelling is **εἶδεν**; the scribe wrote ι for ει, because by then they
sounded the same.

## Other pronunciations you will hear

No one has heard Plato speak, so every pronunciation is a choice. The one in this guide is
pieced together from ancient grammarians, spelling mistakes in inscriptions, and puns: one
comic poet has a fool "saying *bē bē* like a sheep", which shows η was not yet "ee".

- **Erasmian**, the traditional school pronunciation (after Erasmus, 1528). Close to this
  guide's, but θ and φ are said as in *<u>th</u>in* and *<u>f</u>un*, and η as the "ay" of
  *h<u>ay</u>*. It varies from country to country.
- **Modern Greek.** Greeks read ancient texts with today's sounds, as in the table above.

## Practice

**1. Transliterate, remembering the breathings.**

ὕδωρ · ἱππόδρομος · Ἑλλάς · ῥυθμός · ἀρχή · ὁρίζων · ὀρφανός

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- *hydōr* (water)
- *hippodromos* (horse-racecourse): *hippodrome*
- *Hellas* (Greece)
- *rhythmos*: *rhythm*
- *arkhē* (beginning, rule): *archaic*, *monarchy*
- *horizōn* (bounding): *horizon*
- *orphanos*: *orphan*. Smooth breathing, so no *h*.
</details>

**2. What makes these words differ?**

(a) ὅρος and ὄρος · (b) τίς; and τις · (c) θεός in ὁ θεός. but θεὸς in θεὸς ἦν

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- (a) The breathing: *horos* "boundary", *oros* "mountain".
- (b) The accent: "who?" (always accented, and here with a question mark) against the
  enclitic "someone".
- (c) Nothing: it is the same word. Before a full stop the acute stays; when another word
  follows directly, an acute on the last syllable becomes a grave.
</details>

**3. An argument about one letter.** During the plague at Athens (430 BCE), Thucydides says,
people remembered an old verse: "A Dorian war will come, and a λοιμός with it." Some insisted
the verse had really said λιμός ([*History of the Peloponnesian War* 2.54](read:tlg0003.tlg001:2.54)). λοιμός means
"plague", λιμός "famine". How did the two words sound in Thucydides' Athens? How do they
sound in Modern Greek?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

In Thucydides' Athens they were distinct: *loi-mos* with the diphthong οι, and *lī-mos* with
a long ι. In Modern Greek, οι and ι have both become "ee", so the two words sound exactly
alike: *limós*.
</details>
