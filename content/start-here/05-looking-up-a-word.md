# Looking up a word

What the three lookup links in the reader do, which dictionary to use, and one real word
looked up from start to finish.

## The three links in the reader

Click any Greek word in the reader and a small box offers three lookups. Each does a
different job:

| Link | What it does | Use it to |
|---|---|---|
| **Logeion** (University of Chicago) | Searches many dictionaries at once. It accepts any form of a word: type πεπόνθατε and it answers "Parsed as a form of: πάσχω". | read the dictionary entry |
| **Perseus** Word Study Tool (Tufts University) | Analyses a form: πεπόνθατε is perfect indicative active, second person plural, of πάσχω. It often offers several analyses; pick the one that fits the sentence. | find out what form a word is |
| **Wiktionary** | A volunteer-written dictionary with tables of every form, the word's history, and its Modern Greek descendant. Quality varies from entry to entry. | see all the forms at once |

> **Start with Logeion.** If you cannot tell which form you are looking at, ask Perseus.

## Which dictionary

Greek dictionaries are called **lexicons**. Each was made for a different kind of Greek. On
Logeion, the free ones appear together; choose by what you are reading:

| You are reading… | Start with | For more depth |
|---|---|---|
| Attic prose or drama | the Middle Liddell (free) or the *Cambridge Greek Lexicon* | LSJ (free) |
| Homer | Autenrieth (free) | Cunliffe (free), then LSJ |
| Herodotus, lyric, Hellenistic poetry | LSJ | Montanari |
| The New Testament | Abbott-Smith (free) | BDAG |
| The Church Fathers | LSJ | Lampe |

<details><summary>About these dictionaries</summary>

- **LSJ:** Liddell–Scott–Jones, *A Greek–English Lexicon* (9th edition 1940; *Revised
  Supplement* 1996). The great standard dictionary, from Homer to late antiquity, with the
  passages for every meaning. Heavy going for beginners, with its abbreviations and
  Victorian English. Free on Logeion and Perseus.
- **The Middle Liddell:** *An Intermediate Greek–English Lexicon* (1889). A students'
  abridgement of Liddell and Scott: the main meanings, few references. For Attic prose it is
  often the most useful free dictionary.
- **The *Cambridge Greek Lexicon*** (2021, edited by James Diggle). Homer to Plutarch, in
  modern English, with principal parts for verbs. The most learner-friendly large
  dictionary; in print only.
- **Montanari:** *The Brill Dictionary of Ancient Greek* (2015). About 140,000 headwords, to
  the 6th century CE and beyond, drawing on papyri and inscriptions too. In print and by
  subscription.
- **Autenrieth:** *A Homeric Dictionary* (English translation 1876). Compact and friendly,
  with small drawings of ships, weapons and armour. Free.
- **Cunliffe:** *A Lexicon of the Homeric Dialect* (1924). Every sense of every Homeric word.
  Free on Logeion.
- **Abbott-Smith:** *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament* (1922). Small, and a good
  first stop. Free on Logeion.
- **BDAG** (3rd edition, 2000). The scholarly standard for the New Testament and early
  Christian writers. In print or by subscription.
- **Lampe:** *A Patristic Greek Lexicon* (1961–68). The Greek Church Fathers. It covers what
  LSJ lacks, so use it alongside LSJ, not instead of it.
</details>

## A lookup, step by step

Socrates' speech in his own defence opens like this:

> ὅτι μὲν ὑμεῖς, ὦ ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι, **πεπόνθατε** ὑπὸ τῶν ἐμῶν κατηγόρων, οὐκ οἶδα
> "How you, men of Athens, have been affected by my accusers, I do not know."
> ([Plato, *Apology* 17a](read:tlg0059.tlg002:17a))

The word to look up is πεπόνθατε. It is a good one to learn on, because its form gives
almost no clue to its dictionary word.

### 1. Take the word apart

Break it into pieces: **πε · πόνθ · ατε**.

- **πε-** at the front repeats the first consonant, π, with ε. This is **reduplication**
  (step 4), the usual sign of a **perfect**: "have done".
- **-ατε** at the end is a perfect ending for "you" (plural). Compare λελύκατε "you have
  loosened".
- **πονθ-** is left in the middle. No dictionary word begins with it.

So it means "you (plural) have …", but we do not yet know which verb.

### 2. Let a tool find the dictionary word

Click the word and choose **Logeion**. It reports: *"Could not find πεπόνθατε in Logeion
dictionaries. Parsed as a form of: πάσχω."* **Perseus** adds the full analysis: perfect,
indicative, active, second person plural, of **πάσχω**.

The dictionary word is **πάσχω** *paskhō*. Its principal parts are πάσχω, πείσομαι, ἔπαθον,
πέπονθα: none of the letters π-ο-ν-θ appears in πάσχω, which is why guessing would have
failed.

### 3. Check the form in the entry

LSJ's entry opens with the verb's forms, each with a place where it occurs:

> πάσχω, Il. 20.297, etc.: … fut. πείσομαι Od. 2.134, etc.; …
> aor. ἔπᾰθον Il. 9.492, etc.: pf. πέπονθα Od. 13.6, etc.

Read it piece by piece:

- **πάσχω, Il. 20.297**: the headword, found in the *Iliad*, book 20, line 297.
- **fut. πείσομαι Od. 2.134**: the future, found in the *Odyssey*.
- **aor. ἔπᾰθον**: the aorist. The little cup over α tells you it is short.
- **pf. πέπονθα Od. 13.6**: **the perfect, where our word comes from.** πέπονθ-α "I have
  suffered" gives πεπόνθ-ατε "you have suffered".

Skip everything else in this block for now; the abbreviations are listed at the end of this
step.

### 4. Choose the meaning

LSJ numbers the meanings I, II, III, with 1, 2, 3 for sub-senses:

- **I.** "have something done to one, suffer, opp. do" (*opp.* means "opposed to"): πάσχω is
  the opposite of doing. πάσχω τι ὑπό τινος is "to be treated so and so by another", which
  is our sentence's construction: ὑπὸ τῶν ἐμῶν κατηγόρων, "by my accusers".
- **II. 2.** "of the influence of passion or feeling, to be affected in a certain way". Here
  LSJ quotes *our sentence*, from Plato's *Apology* 17a.

Sense II. 2 is the one we want. Socrates does not mean the jurors have "suffered" in the
English sense: the prosecution's speeches have *worked on* them.

### 5. Put it together

> "How you, men of Athens, have been affected by my accusers, I do not know."

> **Editions differ.** LSJ quotes the sentence as ὅ τι ("in what way"), while the edition in
> the app prints ὅτι. Small differences like this are common: dictionaries quote the
> editions that were standard when they were made.

## A family of words

Once you know πάσχω and its stem παθ-, a family opens up:

- πάθος *pathos* "what happens to one; suffering; emotion": English *pathos*, *pathetic*,
  *sympathy* (συμπάθεια, "feeling with").
- Aeschylus sums up the law of Zeus as πάθει μάθος, "learning through suffering"
  ([*Agamemnon* 177](read:tlg0085.tlg005:177)).

The same μαθ- root, "learning", gives this app its name: **Μάθησις** *Mathēsis*, "learning".

## Practice: find the dictionary form

Give the dictionary form of each word, and say what you think the form is. The first
three come from *Iliad* 1.1–3, the next three from *Odyssey* 1.3, the next three from the
*Apology* sentence above, and the last from Aristotle.

ἄειδε · ἔθηκε · ψυχάς · ἴδεν · ἔγνω · ἀνθρώπων · ἄνδρες · κατηγόρων · οἶδα · ἔχει

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. ἄειδε → **ἀείδω** "sing" (Attic ᾄδω): the command "sing!"
2. ἔθηκε → **τίθημι** "put, make, cause": aorist, "(it) caused". The subject is the wrath of line 1; the augment ἐ- is a clue.
3. ψυχάς → **ψυχή, -ῆς, ἡ** "soul, life": accusative plural.
4. ἴδεν → **ὁράω** "see": aorist, "he saw". The Attic aorist is εἶδον; Homer often leaves off the augment. One of the irregular verbs from step 4.
5. ἔγνω → **γιγνώσκω** "come to know": aorist, "he learned".
6. ἀνθρώπων → **ἄνθρωπος, -ου, ὁ** "human being": genitive plural, "of men".
7. ἄνδρες → **ἀνήρ, ἀνδρός, ὁ** "man": vocative plural, "men!"
8. κατηγόρων → **κατήγορος, -ου, ὁ** "accuser": genitive plural. The related κατηγορία gave English *category*.
9. οἶδα → **οἶδα** "I know". It is listed under itself: in form it is a perfect, but it means "I know" in the present.
10. ἔχει → **ἔχω** "have, hold": present, "(he) has".
</details>
