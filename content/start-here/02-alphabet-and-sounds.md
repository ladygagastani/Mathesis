# The alphabet and its sounds

The Greek alphabet has 24 letters. Many will look familiar, because the Romans borrowed
the alphabet from Greeks in Italy, and our own letters come from theirs. A few letters are
false friends: Greek ρ is *r*, not *p*, and Greek η is a vowel, not *n*. An evening's
practice is enough to learn them. This page gives each letter a sound, then shows how
letters combine.

## Three ways to pronounce Ancient Greek

Nobody has heard Plato speak, so every pronunciation is a choice. You will meet three:

- **Reconstructed Classical Attic** (also called "restored" pronunciation). This is our
  best estimate of how educated Athenians spoke around 400 BCE. It is pieced together from:
  - spelling mistakes in inscriptions
  - what ancient grammarians said about sounds
  - how Greek words were spelled in Latin and the other way round
  - puns, and the rhythm of verse

  One comic poet, for instance, has a fool going along "saying *bē bē* like a sheep",
  which tells us η was not yet the *ee* of Modern Greek. The standard account is
  W. Sidney Allen's *Vox Graeca* (3rd edition, 1987).
- **Erasmian.** The traditional school pronunciation, named after Erasmus, who argued in 1528
  that Ancient Greek should not be read with the sounds of the Greek of his own day. It keeps
  the letters apart but varies from country to country: English, German and Dutch
  classrooms each sound different.
- **Modern Greek.** Greeks naturally read ancient texts with today's sounds. The result is
  alive and fluent, but six different spellings all sound *ee* (page 3 explains why), vowel
  length disappears, and with it the rhythm that Greek verse is built on.

**Recommendation:** learn the reconstructed Attic sounds. They explain why words are spelled
as they are, they let you hear the metre of Homer and tragedy (the app's Meter lens depends on
vowel length), and they keep apart sounds such as η and ε, or ο and ω, that the grammar
keeps apart. The only letters most learners find hard are θ, φ and χ. If you cannot yet
manage them, saying them like the *th* in *thin*, the *f* in *fun* and the *ch* in Scottish
*loch* is a widely accepted compromise, because it still keeps them apart from τ, π and κ.
All pronunciations in these pages are the reconstructed ones.

## The letters

In the "Classical Attic" column, *short* and *long* mean how long the vowel is held. The
section after the table explains this.

| Letter | Name | Written as | Classical Attic (recommended) | Erasmian (English schools) | Modern Greek |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Α α | alpha | a | short: the first *a* of "aha"; long: *a* in "father" | *a* in "father" | *a* in "father" |
| Β β | beta | b | *b* in "bed" | *b* | *v* in "van" |
| Γ γ | gamma | g | *g* in "go"; before γ, κ, ξ, χ it is *ng* as in "sing" | *g* | a soft, throaty *g*; before *e* and *i* sounds, *y* in "yes" |
| Δ δ | delta | d | *d* in "dog" | *d* | *th* in "this" |
| Ε ε | epsilon | e | *e* in "pet" (always short) | *e* in "pet" | *e* in "pet" |
| Ζ ζ | zeta | z | *zd* in "wisdom" | *dz* in "adze", or *z* | *z* in "zoo" |
| Η η | eta | ē | a long, open *e*: the *e* of "bed", held | *ay* in "hay" | *ee* in "see" |
| Θ θ | theta | th | *t* with a puff of breath, as in "hothouse" | *th* in "thin" | *th* in "thin" |
| Ι ι | iota | i | short: *i* in French *petit*; long: *ee* in "see" | *i* in "machine" | *ee* in "see" |
| Κ κ | kappa | k | *k* in "skin" (no puff) | *k* | *k* |
| Λ λ | lambda | l | *l* | *l* | *l* |
| Μ μ | mu | m | *m* | *m* | *m* |
| Ν ν | nu | n | *n* | *n* | *n* |
| Ξ ξ | xi | x | *ks* in "box" | *ks* | *ks* |
| Ο ο | omicron | o | a short *o*, as in Italian *otto* (always short) | *o* in "not" | *o* in "not" |
| Π π | pi | p | *p* in "spin" (no puff) | *p* | *p* |
| Ρ ρ | rho | r | a rolled *r*, as in Italian or Spanish | *r* | a tapped *r* |
| Σ σ ς | sigma | s | *s* in "sun"; before β, γ, δ, μ like *z* | *s* | *s* |
| Τ τ | tau | t | *t* in "stop" (no puff) | *t* | *t* |
| Υ υ | upsilon | y (u in pairs) | French *u* in *tu*, German *ü*: say *ee* with rounded lips | French *u*, or *oo* | *ee* in "see" |
| Φ φ | phi | ph | *p* with a puff, as in "top-hat" | *f* in "fun" | *f* in "fun" |
| Χ χ | chi | kh | *k* with a puff, as in "blockhead" | *ch* in "loch" | *ch* in "loch"; before *e* and *i* sounds, like the *h* in "huge" |
| Ψ ψ | psi | ps | *ps* in "lapse" | *ps* | *ps* |
| Ω ω | omega | ō | a long, open *o*: *aw* in British "saw" | *o* in "tone" | *o* in "not" |

**The puff of breath.** Hold your hand in front of your mouth and say "pin", then "spin". The
first *p* comes with a puff of air, the second without. Greek writes these as two different
letters: π is the *p* of "spin", φ is the *p* of "pin", with a stronger puff. In the same way
τ and θ are two kinds of *t*, and κ and χ two kinds of *k*. Sounds with the puff are called
**aspirated** (from Latin *aspirare*, "to breathe on"). The Romans heard them as a letter plus
*h* and spelled them *th*, *ph*, *ch*. That is why English writes *theatre*, *philosophy*,
*chorus*, and why the *ch* in *chorus* sounds like *k*.

## Vowels and vowel length

Greek has seven vowel letters: α ε η ι ο υ ω. Besides its quality (*a*, *e*, *o*…), a vowel has a
**length**: a long vowel is held about twice as long as a short one. English does not use
length this way, so it needs practice, but it matters in Greek:

- **ε and ο are always short.** **η and ω are always long.** Each pair is a short and a long
  version of a similar sound. Greek spells the difference, so it can change meaning:
  ἔθος *ethos* "custom, habit" and ἦθος *ēthos* "character" (the source of *ethics*) are
  different words.
- **α, ι and υ can be either.** The letter is the same, so the spelling does not tell you
  which. Books for learners, and dictionaries in brackets, mark length with a line on top for
  long (ᾱ ῑ ῡ, called a **macron**) and a small cup for short (ᾰ ῐ ῠ, a **breve**). The big
  Liddell–Scott dictionary prints ἔπᾰθον "I suffered", telling you the α is short.
- **Clues without a macron:**
  - A vowel with a circumflex accent (ᾶ, page 3) is always long.
  - A vowel with an iota written under it (ᾳ) is always long.
  - In Attic, α right after ε, ι or ρ at the end of a noun is usually long: χώρᾱ *khōra*
    "land".

Length also carries grammar. λύομεν *lyomen* means "we set free", and λύωμεν *lyōmen* means
"let us set free". Only the length of one vowel tells them apart.

## Diphthongs

A **diphthong** is two vowel sounds glided together in a single syllable, like the *oi* in
English "coin" or the *ow* in "how". In Greek the second part is always ι or υ.

| Diphthong | Written as | Classical Attic sound | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| αι | ai | *ai* in "aisle" | καί *kai* "and" |
| ει | ei | a long, closed *e*, like French *é* held long. It is not the same sound as η. | εἰμί *eimi* "I am" |
| οι | oi | *oi* in "coin" | οἶκος *oikos* "house" |
| υι | ui | French *u* gliding into *ee*, as in French *lui* | υἱός *huios* "son" |
| αυ | au | *ow* in "how" | αὐτός *autos* "self" |
| ευ | eu | *e* of "pet" gliding into *oo* | εὖ *eu* "well" (as in *eulogy*) |
| ηυ | ēu | the same, with a longer first part | ηὔδα *ēuda* "he spoke" ([*Odyssey* 1.213](read:tlg0012.tlg002:1.213)) |
| ου | ou | *oo* in "food" (by Plato's time a single long vowel) | οὐ *ou* "not" |

**The long diphthongs ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ.** These began as a long vowel plus ι (*āi*, *ēi*, *ōi*). Over
time the ι stopped being pronounced. Much later, medieval scribes wrote it as a tiny letter
under the vowel: this is the **iota subscript**. Read ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ simply as long ā, ē, ō. In
capital letters the iota is written beside the vowel instead (the **iota adscript**): ᾠδή
*ōidē* "song" (the source of *ode*) becomes ΩΙΔΗ. You will meet these mostly in word
endings, as in τῷ λόγῳ *tōi logōi* "to the word" and τῇ χώρᾳ *tēi khōrāi* "to the land".

**Spotting a diphthong.** Breathing and accent marks (page 3) sit on the *second* letter of a
diphthong: οἶκος, αὐτός, εἰμί. If a mark sits on the second of two vowels, those two vowels
are a diphthong.

**Diaeresis: when two vowels are *not* a diphthong.** Two dots over ι or υ (ϊ, ϋ) tell you
to pronounce that vowel separately, as in English *naïve*. The *Iliad* has three in its first
three lines:
- Πηληϊάδεω *Pē-lē-i-a-deō* "son of Peleus" (1.1)
- Ἄϊδι *A-i-di* "to Hades" (1.3)
- προΐαψεν *pro-i-ap-sen* "sent forth" (1.3)

Without the dots you would read -ηι-, αι and οι as diphthongs.

## Consonants that trip beginners

- **γ before γ, κ, ξ, χ is *ng*.** ἄγγελος *angelos* "messenger" gives English *angel*;
  ἄγκυρα *ankyra* gives *anchor*; σφίγξ *sphinx*; ἔλεγχος *elenkhos* "refutation" (Socrates'
  method of cross-examination).
- **Three letters, two sounds each.** ζ (*zd*), ξ (*ks*) and ψ (*ps*) are **double
  consonants**. In verse they count as two consonants. Examples: ζῷον *zōion* "animal" (as in
  *zoo*), ξένος *xenos* "stranger, guest" (as in *xenophobia*), ψυχή *psykhē* "soul".
- **θ, φ, χ are aspirated,** as described above, not *th*, *f* and *ch*, at least in
  Plato's day.
- **Two forms of sigma.** σ inside a word, ς at the end: σοφός *sophos* "wise". Some
  editions, especially of papyri, use a single crescent-shaped sigma ϲ everywhere.
- **Double consonants are held.** λλ, ττ, σσ, ρρ are held longer, as in English "book-case":
  Ἀχιλλεύς *Akhilleus*, θάλαττα *thalatta* "sea".
- **σ can sound like *z*.** Before β, γ, δ, μ it was voiced: κόσμος "order, world" sounded
  like *kozmos*.

## Practice

**1. Read aloud,** then check the transliteration and the notes below.

θέατρον · φιλοσοφία · ἄγγελος · ζῷον · ψυχή · ξένος · χάος · κόσμος · αὐτός · οὐρανός · ηὔδα

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- θέατρον *theatron*: θ = *t* + puff.
- φιλοσοφία *philosophia*: both φ are *p* + puff.
- ἄγγελος *angelos*: γγ = *ng-g*.
- ζῷον *zōion*: say *zdō-on*; the subscript ι is silent.
- ψυχή *psykhē*: υ like French *u*.
- ξένος *xenos*: *ks*.
- χάος *khaos*: two syllables, *kha-os*. αο is not a diphthong, because a diphthong always ends in ι or υ.
- κόσμος *kosmos*: sounds like *koz-mos*.
- αὐτός *autos*: *ow*.
- οὐρανός *ouranos* "sky": ου = *oo*.
- ηὔδα *ēuda*: long *ē* gliding into *u*.
</details>

**2. Diphthong or not?** For each word, say whether the vowels in question form a diphthong.

οἶκος · προΐαψεν · αὐτός · υἱός · Ἄϊδι · θεός · ᾠδή · Πηληϊάδεω

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- οἶκος: οι is a diphthong (the accent sits on its second letter).
- προΐαψεν: not a diphthong; the diaeresis separates ο and ι.
- αὐτός: αυ is a diphthong.
- υἱός: υι is a diphthong.
- Ἄϊδι: not a diphthong (diaeresis).
- θεός: not a diphthong. εο cannot be one, because diphthongs end in ι or υ. Say *the-os*, two syllables.
- ᾠδή: ῳ is a long diphthong; read it as long ō.
- Πηληϊάδεω: not a diphthong (diaeresis).
</details>

**3. Transliterate,** then find the English word each one gave us.

βιβλίον · ἄγκυρα · σφίγξ · χορός · κύκλος · ἠχώ · θρόνος

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

- *biblion* (book): *Bible*, *bibliography*
- *ankyra*: *anchor*
- *sphinx*: *sphinx*
- *khoros*: *chorus*, *choir*
- *kyklos* (circle): *cycle*
- *ēkhō*: *echo*
- *thronos* (seat): *throne*
</details>

**4. Say these two words aloud:** λύομεν and λύωμεν. What separates them? Would a Modern
Greek speaker hear any difference?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Only vowel length separates them. In Classical Attic the ο is short and the ω long, so
*lyomen* "we set free" and *lyōmen* "let us set free" sound different. In Modern Greek both
are pronounced *líomen*: vowel length has disappeared (see page 3).
</details>

## For review (not for publication)
- **The sheep.** The line comes from the comic poet Cratinus: a fool goes about "saying βῆ βῆ like a sheep" (fr. 43 Kassel–Austin; numbering differs between editions). I paraphrased rather than quoted. Check the fragment number before citing it anywhere.
- **Allen's example words.** "Top-hat", "hothouse" and "blockhead" are Allen's own illustrations in *Vox Graeca*, as far as I recall. Confirm, and credit him if you keep them.
- **ει and ου.** The page gives their sounds as in Plato's time: ει a long closed *e*, ου already a long *oo*. Some teachers use [oː] for ου in the 5th century. Allen dates the change to *oo* to around the 4th century BCE.
- **Iota subscript.** "Medieval scribes" is deliberately vague. The subscript is usually dated to about the 12th century. The ι had stopped being pronounced by the later Hellenistic period.
- **ζ as *zd*.** This is the standard reconstruction for Classical Attic, but a minority of scholars argue for *dz*.
- **Pindar's heading.** One heading in the app's Pindar text prints ΣΥΡΑΚΟΥΣΙῼ, with the small iota beside the capital (a third convention). I left it out to keep the explanation simple. It could be added as an example.
