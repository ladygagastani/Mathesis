# Which dictionary for what

Greek dictionaries are usually called **lexicons** (from λεξικόν *lexikon*, "of words").
There is no single best one. Each was made for a different period of Greek and a
different kind of reader. Here is what each is for, and when to reach for it.

## The great general lexicon

**LSJ: Liddell–Scott–Jones, *A Greek–English Lexicon*.** The standard large dictionary of
Ancient Greek in English. Henry Liddell and Robert Scott published the first edition in 1843.
The ninth edition, revised by Sir Henry Stuart Jones with Roderick McKenzie, appeared in
parts and was completed in 1940. A *Revised Supplement* followed in 1996.
- **Coverage:** from Homer to late antiquity, with dense references to the passages where
  each meaning occurs. It deliberately leaves out most Christian and Byzantine Greek.
- **Strengths:** unmatched depth, and every major author is cited.
- **For beginners:** heavy going, with its abbreviations, Victorian English, and meanings
  sorted by grammar before sense. It glosses coarse words in Latin rather than English.
- **Where to find it:** free online at Perseus and at Logeion. Page 6 walks through one
  entry.

**The Middle Liddell: *An Intermediate Greek–English Lexicon* (1889).** An abridgement of
Liddell and Scott for students. It keeps the main meanings and names the authors, but drops
most of the exact references, so entries are short and readable. It is aimed at the authors
most often read in school. Free on Perseus and Logeion. For a beginner reading Attic prose
it is often the most useful free dictionary.

## Newer dictionaries

**The *Cambridge Greek Lexicon* (Cambridge University Press, 2021).** Edited by James Diggle
with a team of five. It began as a project to revise the Middle Liddell and ended as an
entirely new dictionary.
- **Coverage:** Greek literature from Homer to the early 2nd century CE (Plutarch). It
  includes the Gospels and Acts, but not the rest of the New Testament, and not the
  Septuagint.
- **Strengths:** modern English, clearly structured entries, and principal parts for verbs.
  It is the most learner-friendly large dictionary now available.
- **Where to find it:** a two-volume printed book.

**Montanari: *The Brill Dictionary of Ancient Greek* (Brill, 2015).** The English version of
Franco Montanari's Italian *Vocabolario della lingua greca*, translated under the auspices of
Harvard's Center for Hellenic Studies (editors Madeleine Goh and Chad Schroeder).
- **Coverage:** about 140,000 headwords, from the earliest texts to the 6th century CE and
  occasionally later. It draws on papyri and inscriptions as well as literature.
- **Strengths:** broader and more up to date than LSJ, and it lists the principal parts of
  some 15,000 verbs.
- **Where to find it:** in print, and online by subscription.

## For Homer

Homer's Greek has so many special forms and rare words that it has dictionaries of its own.

- **Autenrieth, *A Homeric Dictionary*.** Georg Autenrieth's German school dictionary, in
  the English translation by Robert P. Keep (1876; later revised). Compact and friendly: it
  explains Homeric forms, names the passages for many words, and includes small drawings of
  objects such as ships, weapons and armour. Free on Perseus and Logeion.
- **Cunliffe, *A Lexicon of the Homeric Dialect* (1924).** Richard John Cunliffe's much
  fuller work. It sets out every sense of every word with a great many references.
  Reprinted by the University of Oklahoma Press, and free on Logeion.

## For the New Testament and early Christian Greek

- **BDAG: *A Greek–English Lexicon of the New Testament and Other Early Christian
  Literature*** (3rd edition, University of Chicago Press, 2000). Frederick William Danker's
  revision of the German dictionary by Walter Bauer. The initials stand for Bauer, Danker,
  Arndt and Gingrich, the editors of the earlier English editions.
  - **Coverage:** the New Testament and early Christian writers, with comparisons from the
    Septuagint, papyri and inscriptions.
  - **Standing:** the scholarly standard for New Testament Greek.
  - **Where to find it:** in print, or by subscription.
- **Abbott-Smith, *A Manual Greek Lexicon of the New Testament* (1922).** Older and far
  smaller, but free on Logeion, and a good first stop.
- **Lampe, *A Patristic Greek Lexicon*** (Oxford, 1961–68). G. W. H. Lampe's dictionary of
  the Greek Church Fathers (early Christian writers), from Clement of Rome to about the 9th
  century. It was designed to *supplement* LSJ: it concentrates on words and meanings that
  LSJ lacks, or that took on a Christian sense. So use it alongside LSJ, not instead of it.

## Online tools: the three links in the app

Click any Greek word in the reader and a box offers three lookups. Each does a different job:

- **Logeion** (University of Chicago). Searches many dictionaries at once, including LSJ,
  the Middle Liddell, Autenrieth, Cunliffe, Abbott-Smith and Slater's *Lexicon to Pindar*,
  plus French, German, Dutch and Spanish dictionaries.
  - It accepts inflected forms: type πεπόνθατε and it answers "Parsed as a form of: πάσχω".
  - It shows how common a word is (πάσχω is "the 163rd most frequent word") and gives
    examples from real texts.
  - **Best for:** reading the entry.
- **Perseus Word Study Tool** (Tufts University). Analyses a form: it tells you, for
  instance, that πεπόνθατε is perfect indicative active, second person plural, of πάσχω.
  It often offers *several* analyses, because Greek endings can be ambiguous. Choosing the
  one that fits the sentence is your job. It links to the LSJ and Middle Liddell entries.
  - **Best for:** "what form is this?"
- **Wiktionary.** A volunteer-written dictionary, often excellent for beginners. It gives
  full inflection tables, an etymology, a reconstructed pronunciation for several periods
  (Classical Attic, Koine, Byzantine), and the word's Modern Greek descendant. Quality
  varies from entry to entry, so check anything important against LSJ or the *Cambridge
  Greek Lexicon*.
  - **Best for:** seeing every form of a word at once, and its history.

## Quick guide

| You are reading… | Start with | For more depth |
|---|---|---|
| Attic prose or drama | Middle Liddell (free) or the *Cambridge Greek Lexicon* | LSJ |
| Homer | Autenrieth | Cunliffe, then LSJ |
| Herodotus, lyric, Hellenistic poetry | LSJ | Montanari |
| The New Testament | Abbott-Smith (free) | BDAG |
| The Church Fathers | LSJ | Lampe |
| Any form you cannot identify | Perseus Word Study Tool or Logeion | Wiktionary's tables |
